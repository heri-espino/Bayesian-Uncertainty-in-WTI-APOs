\section{Results}\label{sec:short_results}

\subsection{When does posterior integration matter?}

Figure~\ref{fig:short_mechanism} shows how the PI--PM difference varies across the synthetic design. The largest differences occur with short estimation windows, high volatility, long maturities, few realized fixings, and pronounced price curvature. Across the 10 million synthetic histories, the maximum observed absolute difference is 0.1565 for a normalized \$100 forward, and the largest within-cell 99th percentile is 0.1273. These extremes exceed the differences observed in the October-2026 sample. The sample maximum depends on the number of simulated histories; the within-cell percentile is less sensitive to rare simulated outcomes.

The second-order approximation tracks the simulated differences closely: the correlation between cell-level mean PI--PM differences and the curvature--variance term is 0.9995. Through-origin slopes are also close to one in the most extreme settings. These results support posterior variance and price curvature as the principal local determinants of the difference.

The PI--PM difference generally declines as the share of realized fixings increases. The relationship is not strictly monotone: in Panel C, the 252-day case initially rises because partial fixing changes both the remaining volatility exposure and local price curvature. The difference nevertheless tends toward zero as the averaging period ends.

\subsection{Posterior integration in the WTI sample}

The sample of options expiring in October 2026 contains 310 contract-date observations across 12 valuation dates from August 25 to September 10, 2026. Table~\ref{tab:short_october} shows nearly identical pricing errors for PI and PM (RMSE 0.5177 and 0.5183, respectively). Their mean absolute price difference is only 0.000745 dollars per barrel.

The central 95\% posterior interval for conditional model prices has mean width 0.2733 dollars per barrel, approximately 367 times the mean absolute PI--PM difference. The nonzero dispersion of conditional model prices therefore
coexists with an almost unchanged integrated point price.
This interval is not an empirical forecast-error interval or
evidence of executable economic value. Both methods underprice
the reported October settlement proxies on average.

\subsection{Historical versus option-implied volatility}

Table~\ref{tab:short_forward} reports date-ordered conditional pricing errors on 2,381 contract-date observations over only 15 target dates. The historical PI benchmark has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090, compared with 0.1075 and 0.1594 for the previous-day APO implied-volatility smile. The expanding earlier-date smile performs similarly. Both specifications reduce observed settlement-proxy errors for all seven expiries in the saved date-ordered design, with exploratory valuation-date cluster bootstrap intervals for the error differences below zero in each expiry (Figure~\ref{fig:short_forward_bootstrap}).

Rolling-window, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) benchmarks do not close the pricing-error gap (Online Supplement Section S1 and Table S1). In particular, GARCH gives MAE 2.6730 and RMSE 3.4441 on the same observations.

Panel B isolates 2,366 observations with an earlier implied volatility for the same contract. Carrying that volatility forward yields lower pooled errors than the fitted previous-day smile (MAE 0.0992 versus 0.1074). This same-support negative result shows that the advantage
over historical volatility does not require fitting an IV smile.
Its 15 target dates do not support a general claim that
persistence always outperforms a fitted smile.

\subsection{Cross-instrument validation using vanilla-WTI options}

The cross-instrument comparison replaces APO-implied volatility with estimates from standard monthly WTI futures options. Table~\ref{tab:short_lo} uses 253 October-expiry APO contract-date observations across 10 valuation dates. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. A previous-day vanilla-WTI implied-volatility specification based on LO options lowers these errors to 0.1559 and 0.2599. The corresponding previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1676 and RMSE 0.2686.

Valuation-date cluster bootstrap results support the improvement over historical volatility. Differences between LO-implied and APO-implied specifications are small and do not establish consistent superiority by MAE and RMSE. The cross-instrument test instead shows that the advantage persists when implied volatility comes from another WTI option family.


\subsection{Extended conditional settlement-mark evidence}

The separate Databento analysis provides a longer-date check of the
\emph{volatility-input ranking}, not an additional sample from the
earlier path-dependent Curran design. Table~\ref{tab:remef_databento}
reports errors for the same observations classified
as actual settlements by the data provider. The previous-date
AAO implied-volatility benchmark gives an MAE of \$0.2184 per
barrel, versus \$0.8040 for posterior integration of the
historical parity-forward-change volatility proxy. Both estimates
are evaluated within the same reduced-form terminal-average
approximation. Posterior integration itself changes historical
proxy pricing little: the PM MAE is \$0.8068.

The observed discrepancy is consistent with the mismatch between
physical-style changes in a \emph{conditional expected average}
and the option-market-implied dispersion of the \emph{terminal average}.
A retrospective near-the-money inversion on the evaluation date
gives mean implied volatility 30.97\% across 1,689 expiry-date
groups, compared with a Bayesian plug-in price-equivalent volatility
of 26.68\% and lagged AAO option-implied volatility of 30.88\%.
The target-date inversion is used \emph{only to diagnose} pricing
errors after scoring, never as an input to forecasts. These
numbers do not identify a volatility risk premium.

Table~\ref{tab:remef_bayes_iv} isolates the more demanding question:
does a Bayesian market-volatility forecast outperform a simple
persistence benchmark when both use the same prior option-market
information? On 47,718 observations classified as actual settlements across
88 dates in 2025--September 2026, it does not. The unchanged
lagged-IV method has slightly lower MAE and RMSE; price integration
is almost indistinguishable from the matched Bayesian plug-in
price. The difference in equal-date daily MAE between unchanged
lagged IV and Bayesian market-IV PI is -\$0.00100/bbl,
with a 95\% calendar-month-block bootstrap interval of
[-\$0.00331,\$0.00082] for the Bayesian improvement.
The interval crosses zero. These small gaps should not be
presented as economically significant advantages.

The separate exercise assessing \emph{pricing-error interval coverage}
uses 89,696 common actual settlement observations on 174 evaluation dates
after historical warm-up. In the inspected 2025--September 2026
subset, the Bayesian residual model, attached to the Bayesian
market-IV PI point forecast, attains 86.43\% coverage for
nominal 90\% intervals and 90.11\% coverage for nominal
95\% intervals. Corresponding date-balanced rolling empirical
quantile coverages are 84.72\% and 90.66\%. Proper-score
month-block comparisons do not establish a consistent Bayesian
advantage. The model experiences substantial nonstationarity:
nominal 90\% Bayesian intervals cover only 59.0\% and
56.9\% of targets in March and April 2026, respectively.
These figures concern \emph{settlement-mark error intervals};
they do not contradict the earlier much wider posterior
\emph{conditional model-price} bands, which target a different
random quantity.
