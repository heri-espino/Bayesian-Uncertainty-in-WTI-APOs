# Volatility Information versus Posterior Integration in WTI Asian Options

## Provisional English abstract — REMEF structured sequence

**Objective.** We examine whether integrating uncertainty about volatility materially changes WTI average-price option values compared with changing the source of volatility information. **Methods.** We combine an established price-curvature expansion, a ten-million-history synthetic experiment, a futures-based Asian-option valuation study, and a separate conditional settlement-mark benchmark. **Results.** In 310 original contract-date observations, the mean absolute posterior-integrated versus plug-in price difference is USD 0.000745/bbl. In another original sample of 2,381 observations over only 15 valuation dates, historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE USD 2.6187/bbl versus USD 0.1075/bbl for earlier option-implied volatility. In a methodologically distinct settlement-mark study, modeling lagged implied volatility with Bayesian updating does not reliably beat simple persistence. **Recommendations.** Compare volatility inputs and posterior-integration effects separately. **Limitations.** Source timing, small original date samples, model-generated settlement marks and contemporaneous parity anchors constrain interpretation. **Originality.** We organize contract-specific comparisons around distinct pricing and uncertainty estimands. **Conclusions.** Under the specified models, volatility information matters more for point-price errors than integrating the tested posterior volatility distributions.

> **Abstract caveat:** original earlier-IV contract-level rows and source-vintage details are not independently cleared. The present wording is an editorial candidate, not certified evidence. Verify final word count and write Spanish equivalent after final scientific claims are frozen.

## 1. Introduction — candidate prose

An average-price option is valued from a series of underlying prices, not only the price at expiration. For a crude-oil producer or consumer, this distinction connects the option to the monthly sequence of futures settlements that enters the contract payoff. Price estimates depend on both a pricing model for that average and information about future price variation. The choice of volatility input can therefore have a practical effect even when the numerical pricing approximation is held fixed.

A Bayesian approach replaces a single estimated volatility with a posterior distribution. One may calculate a posterior-integrated price by averaging conditional option values over that distribution. Alternatively, one may insert the posterior mean volatility into the same conditional pricing function. These estimates generally differ because the pricing function is nonlinear. That observation is not new, however, and does not imply that posterior integration must produce meaningful economic improvements. It is equally important to distinguish a small change in a posterior-averaged **point price** from the sometimes much larger spread of **conditional model prices** across posterior draws.

Existing research provides strong reasons not to interpret these observations as new theoretical contributions. Geometric-average conditioning and Monte Carlo variance reduction are established methods for valuing Asian options (Kemna and Vorst, 1990; Curran, 1994). Guidolin and Timmermann (2003) demonstrate that Bayesian learning can alter option-price dynamics and pricing performance. Rombouts and Stentoft (2014), however, report that the effect of parameter uncertainty on their S&P 500 option-pricing errors is minor when much data are available. Thus the small price effect of parameter integration is **not in itself a novel discovery**. The open empirical issue here is how this effect compares, within a WTI average-price option valuation exercise, to differences arising from the **source of the volatility signal**.

That comparison is relevant to the crude-oil market, where option-derived volatility measures are known to contain information about subsequent realized return volatility (Gilder and Tsiaras, 2020). Nevertheless, an option-implied volatility is a parameter inferred from option marks under a risk-neutral valuation convention, whereas historical futures returns describe realized variation under the physical measure. Their difference cannot automatically be interpreted as a variance risk premium or a universal ranking of estimators. Furthermore, an end-of-day exchange settlement mark may be generated by an exchange valuation model rather than representing an independent executable transaction. The distinction matters particularly for WTI Average Price Options, whose settlement methodology has employed Curran's approximation since May 2020.

This study evaluates three deliberately separate questions. First, how large is the posterior-integrated-minus-plug-in **point-price** correction within a maintained model, and how does its magnitude vary with posterior dispersion and price curvature? Second, when the conditional model and option targets are held comparable, how do volatility inputs estimated from historical returns compare with inputs extracted from earlier WTI option prices? Third, does a Bayesian model of **strictly earlier** option-implied volatility innovations deliver a material gain over simply carrying forward the last observed implied volatility? The final question also motivates a careful distinction between posterior uncertainty in a theoretical price and the historical coverage of intervals for settlement-mark prediction errors.

The empirical evidence has two distinct sources and should not be described as one common sample. The original WTI study uses individual Barchart APO histories, dated CL futures curves and an arithmetic-average option approximation under a maintained one-factor model. It includes an October-**expiry** sample observed during August and September 2026, a forward-evaluation comparison covering 15 distinct target dates, and a separate external check using earlier LO futures-option implied volatilities. A larger Databento AAO archive instead uses a **reduced-form lognormal terminal-average Black approximation** calibrated partly with contemporaneous parity information from disjoint strikes. This second design is a same-day conditional settlement-mark valuation comparison, not a pathwise reconstruction of first-nearby CL fixings or a fully ex-ante market forecast.

The results indicate that posterior integration has only a small effect on the tested point-price estimates, despite nonzero posterior model-price dispersion. Earlier option-implied volatility is associated with substantially lower errors against the observed settlement proxies than the tested historical-volatility inputs in the original WTI comparison. In the separate conditional Databento study, fitting Bayesian dynamics to lagged market-implied volatility produces no reliable pricing improvement over the unchanged lagged-IV benchmark. Residual interval calibration also deteriorates notably in part of 2026. These findings do not establish structural model validity or tradable mispricing. Rather, they illustrate how much an apparently more sophisticated Bayesian layer contributes once the volatility **information set**, comparator and target are stated explicitly.

**Proposed contribution.** We quantify a limited but practically interpretable distinction between posterior integration and volatility information in WTI average-price option valuation, with a synthetic sensitivity analysis, contract-level option-mark comparisons and explicitly separated empirical designs. We also report when Bayesian market-IV forecasting adds no measured benefit beyond a simple persistence benchmark and distinguish conditional model-price uncertainty from observed mark-error uncertainty. The contribution is primarily a bounded empirical comparison; it is **not** an improved Asian-option pricing algorithm, the first Bayesian application to WTI options, or a theoretical discovery about price curvature.

## 2. Closest-literature comparison — candidate REMEF state-of-the-art section

**Asian option computation and commodity modeling.** Kemna and Vorst (1990) motivate average-based options, including crude oil, and develop numerical pricing with variance reduction. Curran (1994) derives efficient conditioning-based approximations. Commodity extensions incorporating convenience yields and jumps, such as Ewald, Wu and Zhang (2023), address richer underlying-price dynamics. We do not compete with those structural models: the original empirical exercise deliberately fixes simpler futures dynamics to isolate the influence of volatility estimates.

**Bayesian pricing and the magnitude of parameter uncertainty.** Guidolin and Timmermann (2003) show how learning can shape option prices and hedging; Bayesian option pricing is therefore not new. Rombouts and Stentoft (2014) already observe a small impact from parameter uncertainty on their particular pricing-error comparisons, making it an essential predecessor to our negative integration result. Sagor, Boone and Ghanam (2026) propagate joint Bayesian Hurst/volatility uncertainty to European option-price distributions with illustrative WTI data. Our narrower distinction is between the **mean-value correction**, the **conditional model-price distribution** and the comparison with lagged market-IV signals for **arithmetic-average option marks**.

**Volatility signals, model complexity, and REMEF precedent.** Gilder and Tsiaras (2020) compare crude-oil option-implied measures for forecasting realized crude-oil volatility; our target is a different observable, the price error against WTI AAO settlements under an explicit option-pricing approximation. Cummins and Esposito (2025) empirically test whether adding model complexity improves option performance. Finally, REMEF has published Matías González et al. (2019) on Asian options within optimal consumption and portfolio choice and Ortiz Ramírez and Jiménez Preciado (2026) on CEMEXCPO Asian-option simulation with realized volatility and mean reversion. Neither is evidence that the journal has no Asian-option literature; the distinction lies in our conditional WTI contract and information-source comparison.
