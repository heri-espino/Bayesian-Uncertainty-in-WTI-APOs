\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

WTI Average Price Options (APOs) allow market participants to manage
risk in an arithmetic average of first-nearby crude-oil futures
settlements rather than a single delivery-date price. Their
valuation depends on the option payoff, the futures information
available at the valuation date, and the volatility assumption.
Bayesian methods also allow uncertainty about volatility to enter
conditional theoretical values. But more elaborate posterior
computation need not improve a price if its underlying information
is poorly matched to the derivative being valued.

Asian options depend on a price average rather than a single terminal
price. Geometric-average approaches and conditioning approximations
are long-established tools for computing arithmetic-average
option values \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. REMEF has
published related Asian-option work: \citet{matiasgonzalez2019}
study a stochastic optimal consumption/investment framework with
Asian-option valuation, whereas \citet{ortizjimenez2026} apply
realized volatility and mean reversion to the CEMEXCPO setting.
Neither addresses our narrower comparison of the effect of
integrating a volatility posterior on the \emph{price estimate}
with the effect of using different volatility information.

WTI APOs provide a different test setting. Market-specific
models may account for stochastic convenience yields, jumps
or richer futures dynamics \citep{schwartz1997,shirayatakahashi2011,ewaldwu2023}.
We hold the one-factor option-pricing benchmark fixed in
the original analysis, so the influence of volatility
information is not conflated with changes in the dynamics
of the commodity futures curve. This is deliberately a
controlled comparison, not a search for the most elaborate
structural commodity-price model.

The distinction between historical and option-implied information
is important. \citet{gildertsiaras2020} study forecasts of
\emph{subsequent realized crude-oil volatility} extracted from
option prices. We study errors in pricing WTI
\emph{average-price options} at later valuation dates. The
risk-neutral option-implied dispersion need not equal
a physical-return volatility estimate, especially if
the latter uses a proxy for the wrong underlying random
quantity. Consequently, a large historical-versus-implied
pricing difference by itself neither identifies a variance
risk premium nor demonstrates that Bayesian inference fails.

Our distinction has three parts. First, posterior-integrated
pricing (PI) averages conditional values over the
volatility posterior, whereas posterior-mean plug-in
pricing (PM) evaluates the conditional value at one
posterior mean. Their difference concerns the
\emph{point price}; it does not equal the dispersion of
the conditional model-price distribution. Second, the
source of volatility information is an independent
empirical choice. Third, a posterior distribution for
model parameters and a predictive interval for actual
settlement-mark errors answer different uncertainty
questions and should not be presented interchangeably.

We examine those distinctions in a 10-million-history
synthetic study and in two observational exercises,
each reported with its own pricing assumptions and
information cutoffs. The first uses individual Barchart
APO histories, a CL futures-curve-based one-factor
Asian option approximation, 310 October-expiry
contract-date observations in August--September 2026,
2,381 forward-evaluation observations across 15 dates,
and a 253-observation cross-instrument check using
earlier LO vanilla-WTI implied volatilities. The second
uses a larger Databento AAO settlement archive and a
\emph{different}, reduced-form lognormal
terminal-average pricing approximation. The second
exercise evaluates 97,341 actual-flagged settlement
marks on 189 dates and includes prior-date
market-IV Bayesian forecasting and retrospective
pricing-residual interval calibration.

The central empirical finding is not that a
particular Bayesian prior dominates. In the
original analysis, historical-volatility posterior
integration changes point prices little while
earlier option-implied volatility substantially
reduces the errors of the tested historical
volatility inputs. The distinct longer-date AAO
exercise confirms the importance of the volatility
\emph{information source} but shows no reliable gain
from Bayesian market-IV integration over carrying
forward the previous implied volatility. Its
uncertainty-calibration comparison also reveals
severe undercoverage in part of 2026. These
results are conditional: some settlement
observations may be derived from an exchange
valuation model, and the extended analysis uses
same-day \emph{disjoint-strike} parity inputs.
The two empirical cohorts cannot be pooled as
if they came from one identical model.

Our contribution is therefore a transparent
separation of three effects often grouped under
the label of Bayesian option pricing: posterior
curvature corrections, choice of volatility
signals, and predictive uncertainty calibration.
The paper explains the economic and
measurement limits of each. The next
sections describe the model, the two
independent empirical designs, the
results and their implications.
