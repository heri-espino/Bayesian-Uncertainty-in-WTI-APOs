\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

Volatility is not observed directly and must be estimated or inferred from option prices. In Bayesian option valuation, integrating conditional prices over a volatility posterior accounts for parameter uncertainty, whereas plug-in pricing evaluates the same pricing function at a point estimate \citep{guidolin2003,romboutsstentoft2014,guptareisinger2014}. The practical question is whether posterior integration changes an Asian-option value by enough to matter relative to differences arising from the volatility input itself.

Asian options depend on a price average rather than a single terminal price. Established approximations and simulation methods account for this path dependence \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. REMEF has published related applications: \citet{matiasgonzalez2019} develop a stochastic-control approach to Asian-option valuation and examine Monte Carlo prices using Mexican equity data, while \citet{ortizjimenez2026} value Asian options with realized volatility and mean reversion in a CEMEXCPO application. These studies motivate empirical analysis of volatility assumptions in Asian-option pricing; neither directly compares a posterior-integrated price with a posterior-mean plug-in price for exchange-traded WTI Average Price Options (APOs).

WTI APOs provide a different test setting. Their payoff depends on calendar-month first-nearby futures settlements, including both realized fixings and contracts on the remaining futures curve. Commodity-pricing models can incorporate stochastic convenience yields, jumps, and multiple risk factors \citep{schwartz1997,shirayatakahashi2011,ewaldwu2023}. We deliberately hold a common-factor, constant-volatility futures model fixed so that posterior integration and volatility information can be examined separately.

The distinction between historical and option-implied information is important. \citet{gildertsiaras2020} find that option-implied measures contain information about subsequent realized crude-oil volatility, but their study concerns volatility forecasts, not WTI APO settlement-price errors. Our comparison instead uses only earlier option observations to construct implied-volatility inputs and then reprices APOs against later settlements. Option-implied volatility under our maintained model is not a separately identified risk-neutral diffusion parameter or a direct measure of the variance risk premium.

We investigate three questions. First, under which combinations of historical sample size and option-price curvature does posterior integration differ materially from plug-in pricing? Second, how does that price correction compare with the dispersion of theoretical prices induced by posterior volatility uncertainty? Third, how do historical-volatility estimates and lagged option-implied inputs compare on the same out-of-sample APO observations? The evidence combines a controlled experiment over 10 million simulated return histories, a 310-observation October-2026 posterior-pricing comparison, a 2,381-observation out-of-sample APO panel, and a separate 253-observation check using implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options.

The results indicate that posterior integration can matter in short-sample, high-curvature settings, but it changes the point price very little in the observed October-2026 APO sample. Posterior price dispersion remains substantially larger than the PI--PM correction. In out-of-sample pricing, earlier-date option-implied volatility gives smaller errors than the historical-volatility benchmarks examined. These findings concern the maintained pricing model and available valuation dates; they do not establish a general preference for one pricing model across markets.
