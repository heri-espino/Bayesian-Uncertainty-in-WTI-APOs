\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies a narrow but practically important question in derivative valuation: when volatility is estimated rather than known, does integrating its posterior distribution materially change the value of a path-dependent commodity option relative to conventional plug-in pricing? The framework enforces a separation that is essential for answering this question correctly. Historical parameters are inferred under the physical measure, while derivative values are computed under the risk-neutral measure. In the baseline Black--Scholes setting, uncertainty about the physical drift is therefore not pricing uncertainty.

The synthetic evidence establishes a clear benchmark. In regular settings, full posterior integration and pricing at the posterior mean of volatility can be almost indistinguishable. This outcome follows naturally when posterior dispersion is limited and the option-pricing function is approximately linear over the relevant posterior support. The marginal sigma-mode plug-in is somewhat less stable in the smallest preliminary samples, while MLE remains competitive. These results argue against presenting Bayesian integration as mechanically superior. Its economic value must instead be demonstrated in states where posterior uncertainty interacts with sufficient pricing curvature.

The first contract-consistent WTI computational pilot reaches the same qualitative conclusion. The October-2026 panel contains 310 retained option-date observations across 12 eligible valuation dates. Full-Bayes pooled RMSE is 0.5083, compared with 0.5089 for the posterior-mean plug-in. On the five valuation dates containing at least one positive-volume contract, the corresponding RMSE values are 0.4245 and 0.4253. Restricting further to the six contract-date observations that themselves report positive volume lowers the RMSEs to 0.1783 and 0.1788. Across those six observations, average absolute $|\Delta_{FB,PM}|$ is approximately 0.00063 and the maximum is approximately 0.00110. The integration effect is therefore very small relative to the remaining model-to-market discrepancy within the baseline specification.

That result should not be interpreted as evidence that posterior uncertainty is absent. Rolling volatility posteriors remain non-degenerate, with posterior standard deviations near 0.0112--0.0114 on the positive-volume dates. Rather, the empirical finding is that the current one-factor constant-volatility pricing function is sufficiently smooth over the posterior support that integration and posterior-mean plug-in valuation produce almost the same point price. In that sense, the empirical panel supplies a working-draft quantitative bound on the incremental value of posterior integration under the baseline model.

The liquidity results also sharpen the interpretation of absolute pricing errors. Errors are materially smaller in the strict positive-volume sample than in the full mark-based panel, which indicates that benchmark quality and market participation matter for empirical fit. At the same time, the strict sample contains only six observations, so it does not support a broad ranking of full Bayes, posterior-mode, and MLE rules. The robust conclusion is narrower: full Bayes and posterior-mean pricing are economically close in the current October-2026 pilot.

Several limitations remain material. Historical volatility inference currently uses Yahoo \texttt{CL=F} as a labelled continuous/front-month proxy rather than a contract-reconstructed first-nearby return series. Barchart \texttt{Latest} is treated as an end-of-day settlement proxy rather than an independently verified official CME settlement. Discounting uses an interpolated Treasury par-yield approximation, and the October pilot uses a weekday fixing calendar. In addition, the current contract-consistent panel covers one APO expiry month and was generated with the lower-cost computational preset. These limitations define the next robustness steps rather than invalidating the baseline comparison, because all pricing rules are evaluated under the same data and model specification.

The paper's contribution is therefore conditional rather than universal. For the synthetic benchmark and the current WTI APO pilot, posterior integration does not materially change the point valuation relative to posterior-mean plug-in pricing. Before submission, the multi-date panel should be rerun at the production computational budget. Future extensions should then test whether the conclusion survives broader maturities, richer futures-curve dynamics, stochastic volatility, alternative estimation windows, and larger actively traded samples. If posterior integration becomes important only in those more nonlinear or weakly identified states, the analysis will identify where the additional computation is economically justified. If it remains negligible, the result places a useful empirical bound on the value of carrying parameter uncertainty through this class of commodity-derivative pricing engines.
