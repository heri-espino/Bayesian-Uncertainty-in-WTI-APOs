\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies when posterior uncertainty about volatility materially changes the value of a path-dependent commodity option, and how that within-model effect compares with the volatility state used for risk-neutral valuation. The distinction is important: posterior integration can propagate uncertainty in a specified parameter, but it cannot by itself determine whether the parameter supplied to the pricing model is the state reflected in option markets.

The synthetic evidence gives a clear answer to the first question. Posterior integration is not generically negligible. Across 175,000 simulated histories and 168 WTI-like contract states, the largest observed absolute PI--PM gap reaches 0.1384. The largest gaps occur with short historical samples, high volatility, long remaining horizons, extreme moneyness, and little realized fixing. In those states, the second-order approximation
\[
\frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)\operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D)
\]
tracks the realized integration gap almost one-for-one. Posterior uncertainty matters when dispersion and pricing curvature are simultaneously large.

The observed WTI application lies in a different region of that state space. In the October-2026 baseline, posterior-integrated and posterior-mean plug-in prices are nearly identical: pooled RMSE is 0.5177 versus 0.5183 across 310 observations. High-precision pseudo-random Monte Carlo, randomized Sobol sequences, and Curran conditioning reproduce the sub-cent PI--PM differences with numerical uncertainty several orders of magnitude smaller, so the empirical near-equivalence is not a simulation artifact.

The larger empirical margin is volatility specification. A strict forward-in-time APO experiment spans seven expiries and 2,381 holdouts. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090; prior-date APO smiles reduce those errors to approximately 0.11 MAE and 0.16--0.17 RMSE. That experiment shows that option-implied volatility information is strongly forward-relevant, but because the information is extracted from APOs it does not by itself eliminate a within-family explanation.

The independent vanilla-WTI experiment addresses that limitation directly. Using only prior-date standard monthly WTI option settlements and matching CL futures settlements, the paper constructs an external American-option implied-volatility surface and transfers it to the APO fixing horizon. On 253 October-2026 contract-dates lying inside the observed LO moneyness support, historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. The external previous-day LO surface reduces these to 0.1559 and 0.2599; the corresponding prior-date APO smile gives 0.1676 and 0.2686. Valuation-date cluster bootstraps show a large and stable external-LO improvement over historical volatility, while differences between the external LO and APO surfaces are small. The main conclusion is therefore not that vanilla options dominate APO information, but that the volatility-specification result survives when the risk-neutral state is constructed from a different WTI option family.

Heavy-tailed physical-measure returns improve fit modestly without changing this hierarchy. A Student-$t$ specification produces a wider volatility posterior, but its pricing improvement is much smaller than the gain from an option-informed risk-neutral state. Prior sensitivity is limited once the historical sample is long, and large-scale simulation-based calibration supports the Gaussian posterior implementation.

The central conclusion is conditional rather than universal. Bayesian parameter integration is worth carrying when posterior dispersion meets substantial pricing curvature; the synthetic mechanism map identifies exactly those regimes. In the observed WTI APO application, however, the posterior over historical volatility is sufficiently concentrated and the local pricing map sufficiently smooth that integration is a second-order refinement. The first-order empirical margin is the volatility state supplied to the pricing model. The independent LO validation makes this distinction harder to attribute to circular calibration within the APO family.

Several limitations remain. The pricing dynamics are deliberately one-factor and constant-volatility, so the effective implied-volatility states can absorb volatility risk premia, stochastic volatility, jumps, omitted futures-curve dynamics, and market-marking effects; they are not interpreted as unique structural diffusion coefficients. Historical inference uses a continuous/front-month return proxy, discounting uses a Treasury par-yield approximation, and positive-volume observations remain sparse in the longest-dated APO maturities. The vanilla experiment is also concentrated on the October-2026 fixing structure, although its final 70--120 strike range places 90.4\% of the matched contract-date sample inside observed LO moneyness support.

These limitations point toward a richer next model rather than a different empirical conclusion. Future work can combine independently observed vanilla-WTI surfaces with multi-factor futures dynamics or stochastic volatility and propagate uncertainty in those additional states. The benchmark established here is simpler: Bayesian integration addresses uncertainty conditional on a model, while external option-market information addresses the empirical volatility state. In WTI APOs, the latter is the dominant pricing margin in the observed data.
