\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies a specific consequence of Bayesian parameter uncertainty: how much integrating a posterior for historical volatility changes an Average Price Option point value relative to plugging in the posterior mean. That mean-price correction is distinct from the width of the posterior distribution of theoretical prices and from the choice of volatility information supplied to the pricing model.

The synthetic evidence shows that the integration correction is not generically negligible. Across 175,000 simulated histories and 168 WTI-like contract states, the largest observed absolute PI--PM gap is 0.1384. The largest corrections occur with short historical samples, high volatility, long remaining horizons, extreme moneyness, and little realized fixing. In those states, the second-order approximation
\[
\frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
\operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D)
\]
tracks the realized integration gap closely. Posterior integration can therefore become appreciable when posterior dispersion and volatility curvature are jointly large.

The observed WTI application lies in a different region of that state space. In the October-2026 baseline, posterior-integrated and posterior-mean plug-in RMSE are 0.5177 and 0.5183 across 310 observations. The mean absolute PI--PM correction is only 0.000745, but the mean width of the central 95\% posterior distribution of model prices is 0.2733. Close PI and PM values therefore do not imply that parameter-induced price uncertainty is negligible; they imply that nonlinear averaging shifts the posterior mean price very little in these states. Independent pseudo-random Monte Carlo, randomized Sobol, and Curran calculations resolve this small correction well below its observed magnitude.

The larger empirical difference is between volatility inputs. Across 2,381 strict forward-in-time holdouts, the long-window historical-volatility posterior-integrated benchmark has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090, whereas prior-date APO smiles have MAE near 0.11 and RMSE near 0.16--0.17. Reconstructing the physical return history contract by contract from first-nearby CL settlements changes the historical RMSE only to 3.4451, so this comparison is not driven by Yahoo's continuous-contract roll construction.

The independent vanilla-WTI experiment provides the stronger identification check. On 253 October-2026 contract-dates inside prior LO moneyness support, the historical benchmark has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. The external previous-day LO surface gives 0.1559 and 0.2599, while the corresponding prior-date APO smile gives 0.1676 and 0.2686. The result therefore survives when the volatility input is constructed from a different WTI option family rather than from APO settlements themselves.

The interpretation remains narrower than a pure physical-versus-risk-neutral decomposition. Recent option-informed surfaces differ from the long-window historical benchmark in recency, maturity dependence, strike dependence, and estimation flexibility as well as in information source. The empirical conclusion is consequently that recent option-informed volatility inputs predict the observed APO settlements much better than the maintained long-window constant-volatility benchmark, while more accurate integration of that benchmark's posterior changes point prices very little. The recent-history benchmarks described in Section~\ref{sec:design} are designed to test how much of that advantage is specifically attributable to recency.

Several limitations remain. The pricing dynamics are deliberately one-factor and constant-volatility, so effective implied-volatility states can absorb volatility risk premia, stochastic volatility, jumps, omitted futures-curve dynamics, and market-marking effects. The LO-to-APO volatility transfer is an effective scalar mapping rather than an exact variance identity for the arithmetic average. Discounting uses a Treasury par-yield approximation. Positive-volume observations remain sparse in the longest-dated APO maturities.

The temporal dimension is also short. The seven-expiry forward experiment contains 2,381 contract-date observations but only 15 distinct target dates, and the external LO common-support experiment contains 10 target dates. Valuation-date cluster resampling preserves contemporaneous dependence among contracts observed on the same date, but it does not create additional market histories or automatically remove serial dependence across adjacent dates. Bootstrap improvement frequencies close to one are therefore interpreted as properties of the observed date-cluster resampling design, not as certainty about future samples.

The paper's contribution is thus diagnostic rather than a new option-pricing theory. It separates the posterior-integration mean correction, posterior model-price dispersion, numerical error, and volatility-input specification; maps when the first of these becomes appreciable; and tests the empirical volatility-input result both within the APO family and with independent vanilla-WTI information. A natural next step is to ask whether the option-informed advantage survives the stronger recent-history benchmarks and then, only if needed, to move to richer futures or stochastic-volatility dynamics.
