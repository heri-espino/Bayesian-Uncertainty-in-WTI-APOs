\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We ask how much a WTI Average Price Option value changes when we integrate over the historical-volatility posterior rather than using posterior mean volatility. A second-order approximation relates the PI--PM difference to posterior variance and the curvature of the option price with respect to volatility. The synthetic experiment shows that this difference can be important when the historical sample is short and price curvature is large.

In the observed WTI sample, PI and PM are almost identical, although the posterior distribution of conditional model prices is considerably wider than their difference. Thus a small \(C_{PI}-C_{PM}\) does not imply little uncertainty about the model price.

Changing the volatility input has a much larger effect on empirical pricing errors. On the out-of-sample observations, implied volatility estimated from earlier option prices gives much lower errors than the historical-volatility benchmarks tested. Rolling windows, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH do not achieve comparable results. The improvement does not require a fitted implied-volatility smile, and it remains when volatility is inferred from standard monthly WTI futures options. These findings do not measure a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility.

The paper compares two effects within a fixed option-pricing model rather than proposing a new pricing theory. In this sample, changing from historical to prior option-implied volatility reduces pricing errors much more than switching from PM to PI. The evidence is limited by relatively few market dates, uneven liquidity, and the maintained one-factor model. Studying richer commodity-price dynamics would require a separate comparison.
