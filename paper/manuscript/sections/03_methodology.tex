\section{Methodology and research design}\label{sec:methodology}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Physical inference and risk-neutral pricing}

We model historical WTI returns under the physical measure using geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility. For log return \(R_i\) over interval \(\Delta t\),
\begin{equation}
 R_i\sim
 \mathcal N\!\left[
 \left(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2\right)\Delta t,
 \sigma^2\Delta t
 \right].
 \label{eq:return_likelihood}
\end{equation}
The baseline prior is
\begin{equation}
 \mu\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
 \qquad
 \sigma\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(2,0.1),
 \quad \sigma>0.
 \label{eq:priors}
\end{equation}
For the market data, we sample the posterior using random-walk Metropolis in \((\mu,\log\sigma)\). For the large synthetic experiment, we integrate out \(\mu\) analytically conditional on \(\sigma\). This gives a one-dimensional calculation without MCMC error. The online supplement provides sampler settings, calibration checks, and alternative models.

Valuation is performed separately under the risk-neutral measure. For each unresolved WTI fixing, the maintained futures benchmark is
\begin{equation}
 dF_u=\sigma F_u\,dW_u^Q.
 \label{eq:futures_q}
\end{equation}
The historical drift does not enter the pricing model. Known fixings enter the average as fixed numbers, and only future settlements are simulated. For each future fixing, we use the relevant contract price from the observed CL futures curve. The simple model allows us to compare volatility inputs without also changing the futures dynamics.

We calculate baseline empirical prices by Monte Carlo. For the larger out-of-sample comparisons and checks using constant-volatility inputs, we use Curran's deterministic conditioning approximation \cite{curran1994} under the same one-factor model. Difficult contracts are also checked with high-precision pseudo-random and scrambled-Sobol calculations. Numerical diagnostics are summarized in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} and documented in detail in the supplement.

\subsection{Posterior integration and uncertainty about model prices}

The primary comparison is between
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],
 \qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\bar\sigma),
 \qquad
 \bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D].
 \label{eq:estimators}
\end{equation}
A Taylor expansion around \(\bar\sigma\) gives the central mechanism,
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}
 \approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D).
 \label{eq:taylor_gap}
\end{equation}
The PI--PM difference therefore depends locally on posterior variance and price curvature. By contrast,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{SD}\!\left(C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D\right)
 \approx
 |C^{Q\prime}(\bar\sigma)|
 \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D)},
 \label{eq:price_uncertainty_delta}
\end{equation}
so model-price uncertainty follows a different scale: its standard deviation is locally proportional to the posterior standard deviation of volatility. The PI--PM difference can be small even when the posterior distribution of model prices is wide.

The repeated-sampling benchmark also includes estimates based on the posterior mode and maximum likelihood. These are secondary comparisons. We also test a posterior root mean square (RMS) volatility as another constant-volatility input, not as a posterior-integration method.

\subsection{Synthetic mechanism experiment}

We simulate return histories under \(\mathbb P\) and calculate target option values under \(\mathbb Q\) using the known volatility. The large mechanism map contains 175,000 independent histories and spans short to long estimation samples, annual volatility from 0.10 to 0.80, multiple maturities and moneyness states, and partial-fixing configurations. For each history and contract state, the experiment records the PI--PM gap, posterior variance of volatility, local pricing curvature, and the approximation in Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_gap}.

This experiment tests when posterior averaging changes prices and how well Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_gap} explains the change. It is not a test against market settlements. The supplement reports the complete simulation grid and replication counts.

\subsection{Forward WTI APO validation}

We estimate historical volatility using returns available by each valuation date. We estimate APO implied volatility by finding the input to Curran's approximation that matches each observed option settlement. These implied volatilities are inputs to our pricing model, not structural estimates of risk-neutral diffusion volatility.

We fit implied-volatility smiles using only option observations from earlier dates. One fit uses the most recent earlier date. Another uses all earlier dates with more weight on recent observations. Seven expiries provide 2,381 observations over 15 target dates.

We compare the option-implied results with several historical-volatility estimates: rolling windows of 63, 126, and 252 returns, an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) with a 63-business-day half-life, and a Gaussian GARCH(1,1) forecast matched to the remaining fixing dates. All methods provide volatility inputs to the same pricing model. We also test whether smile fitting adds value. We carry forward each contract's latest earlier implied volatility. We then price it with the current futures curve, discount factor, and known and remaining fixings. GARCH estimation and the conversion of variance forecasts to one constant volatility are described in the supplement.

We compare model prices with end-of-day settlements using mean error, MAE, and RMSE. Contracts on the same date share market conditions, so our bootstrap resamples valuation dates rather than individual contracts. Liquidity restrictions based on positive volume and open interest are reported as robustness checks.

\subsection{Independent vanilla-WTI validation}\label{sec:external_lo_method}

The first out-of-sample comparison estimates implied volatility from earlier APOs. As a separate test, we use standard monthly American WTI futures options (CME product LO). We match LO settlements to the corresponding CL futures settlements and infer volatility with an American Cox--Ross--Rubinstein futures-option tree. The main inversion uses a 160-step tree. The supplement reports checks using different numbers of steps.

We fit the external LO volatility surfaces using only observations earlier than the APO target date. We fit each underlying futures contract separately and evaluate the surface at the APO strike. The APO model requires one volatility, so we combine the maturity-specific LO estimates. Our main method uses a fixing-count weighted RMS value. As a check, we choose a value that matches the variance of the remaining average under the common-factor model. Neither rule is claimed to be an exact representation of market dynamics.

The primary external comparison is restricted to APO observations lying inside the observed prior LO moneyness support for both external specifications. This yields 253 common-support contract-dates over 10 target dates. Same-day APO prices never enter the external volatility construction. This checks whether the improvement over historical volatility remains when implied volatility is estimated from vanilla-WTI options rather than APOs.
