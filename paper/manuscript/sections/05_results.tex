\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

This section reports the completed baseline evidence from the repeated-sampling experiment and the first contract-consistent WTI APO pricing panel. The synthetic results isolate parameter-integration effects when the true volatility is known. The empirical results use October-2026 WTI APO market marks, the contemporaneous Barchart CL term structure, rolling physical-measure posteriors based on information available at each valuation date, and date-specific Treasury discounting.

\subsection{Synthetic evidence: integration versus plug-in}

Table~\ref{tab:synthetic_pilot} reports the initial repeated-sampling comparison. With only 63 daily observations, full Bayes has RMSE 0.469, compared with 0.469 for the posterior-mean plug-in, 0.487 for the marginal sigma-mode plug-in, and 0.470 for MLE. The full-Bayes and posterior-mean prices are effectively indistinguishable in both bias and absolute error. As the historical sample increases to 252 and 1,260 observations, all four methods converge and the difference between full Bayes and posterior-mean plug-in becomes numerically negligible.

The near equality of $\widehat C_{FB}$ and $\widehat C_{PM}$ is economically informative. In this baseline setting, posterior dispersion and curvature are not large enough for Jensen-type effects to generate a meaningful pricing difference. This is consistent with the mechanism in Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_gap}: posterior integration is not expected to produce a premium merely because the analysis is Bayesian. The result also accords with the broader observation that parameter-uncertainty effects may be modest in sufficiently informative samples \cite{romboutsstentoft2014}.

The marginal sigma-mode plug-in performs somewhat less stably in the smallest sample, but the ranking is not monotone at every sample size. The evidence therefore does not support a generic claim that one plug-in estimator is uniformly dominated. The production experiment expands volatility, moneyness, and maturity specifically to identify where the pricing map becomes nonlinear enough for posterior integration to separate from plug-in valuation.

\subsection{Posterior price uncertainty in a baseline case}

For the corrected baseline implementation, the high-precision risk-neutral benchmark is approximately 6.42. A representative posterior fit gives a full-Bayes value near 6.24, a posterior-mean plug-in near 6.24, a marginal sigma-mode plug-in near 6.22, and an MLE plug-in near 6.23. The corresponding central 95\% posterior price interval is approximately $[5.80,6.73]$.

The interval has a different interpretation from the point-estimator comparison. Even when $C_{FB}$ and $C_{PM}$ are almost identical, the push-forward posterior in Equation~\eqref{eq:pushforward} can remain non-degenerate. Thus, ``integration does not change the point price much'' does not imply ``parameter uncertainty is zero.'' The empirical section below preserves this distinction by reporting both pricing-rule differences and model-to-market error.

Trading activity is sparse for many contracts. Across several expiry/type groups, only a small percentage of dates report positive volume even when open interest is positive. This makes transaction-price language inappropriate for the full panel. The market benchmark is therefore an end-of-day Barchart mark, and liquidity is treated as an explicit sample dimension rather than assumed away.

The first completed contract-consistent panel uses the October-2026 APO expiry. Both required futures-curve contracts, \texttt{CLX26} and \texttt{CLZ26}, are available from August 25 through September 10, 2026. Across those 12 eligible valuation dates, the main sample contains 310 option-date observations. Five dates contain at least one option with positive reported volume, producing a 136-observation positive-volume-date sample. Only six individual contract-date observations report positive daily volume themselves. The strict sample is therefore useful as a benchmark for stronger market participation, but it is too small to support broad statistical claims.

Two patterns are immediate. First, full Bayes and the posterior-mean plug-in are extremely close in every sample. The pooled RMSE difference is 0.0006 in the 310-observation sample, 0.0008 on positive-volume dates, and 0.0005 in the strict six-observation positive-volume sample. These differences are small relative to the level of model-to-market error. Second, restricting attention to positive-volume contracts lowers the absolute errors substantially, but the sample shrinks from 310 to six observations. The reduction in error should therefore be interpreted primarily as evidence that benchmark quality and liquidity matter for absolute fit, not as a basis for a strong cross-method ranking.

The all-dates panel also illustrates why ranking methods by one pooled statistic would be misleading. The marginal sigma-mode plug-in has the lowest pooled RMSE in the broadest sample, while full Bayes has the lowest RMSE in the two liquidity-restricted samples. With differences of this magnitude and a very small strict transaction-activity sample, the economically robust statement is the near equivalence of the pricing rules within the baseline model, especially full Bayes and posterior-mean pricing.

\subsection{The full-Bayes/posterior-mean gap}

The main object of interest is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{FB,PM,i,t}=P^{FB}_{i,t}-P^{PM}_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
The six positive-volume contract-date observations make the magnitude of this gap transparent. Their average absolute $|\Delta_{FB,PM}|$ is approximately 0.00063, and the largest absolute gap is approximately 0.00110. By comparison, the full-Bayes MAE in the same six observations is 0.1477. Thus the difference created by posterior integration is more than two orders of magnitude smaller than the typical model-to-market error in this strict sample.

This result is consistent with the Taylor mechanism in Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_gap}. Across the five positive-volume valuation dates, posterior mean volatility remains close to 0.415--0.416 and posterior standard deviation remains near 0.0112--0.0114. The posterior is non-degenerate, but over this range the pricing map is sufficiently smooth that integrating over the posterior and evaluating at its mean produce almost the same point price. The empirical finding therefore supports a narrower conclusion than ``Bayes does not matter'': under the current one-factor constant-volatility APO baseline, posterior integration has little incremental effect on the point valuation relative to posterior-mean plug-in pricing.

\subsection{Interpretation and remaining empirical scope}

The current evidence separates two questions that are easy to conflate. The first is whether the baseline model fits market marks closely. The systematic negative mean errors in Table~\ref{tab:oct26_empirical} indicate that absolute fit is imperfect and may reflect volatility smile/skew, richer futures-curve dynamics, benchmark quality, or other model features omitted from the one-factor specification. The second is whether integrating parameter uncertainty materially improves on a point estimate \emph{within the same model}. The October-2026 panel indicates that this second effect is small.

The result is not yet a statement about all WTI APO maturities or volatility regimes. The strict positive-volume sample contains only six observations, all from the October-2026 expiry and dates before the averaging month begins. Broader conclusions require additional contract-specific CL history, explicit exchange settlement calendars for all averaging months, and a larger set of actively traded option observations. Those extensions are treated as robustness and scope questions rather than prerequisites for reporting the completed baseline panel.
