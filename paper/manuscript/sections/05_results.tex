\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

This section reports four comparisons. We first examine when PI and PM differ in synthetic data and whether a small PI--PM difference can coexist with substantial uncertainty about the model price. We then compare historical volatility with implied volatility estimated from earlier APO observations. Finally, we repeat the volatility-input comparison using standard monthly WTI futures options as an external source.

PI and PM are close in this repeated-sampling experiment, but they need not be close in general. Their difference depends on the volatility posterior and the curvature of the pricing function. The larger synthetic experiment tests this relationship across a wider range of conditions.

\subsection{Where posterior integration matters}

The synthetic experiment contains 175,000 independently simulated return histories and 168 WTI-like contract settings. Its largest absolute PI--PM difference is 0.1384, and the largest 99th percentile within a setting is 0.1271. Both are much larger than the differences of roughly $10^{-3}$ observed in the WTI baseline. Thus, PI and PM are not close simply because the pricing model forces them to be.

The mechanism map normalizes the WTI-like forward level to \$100 per barrel, so the maximum gap of 0.1384 is \$0.1384 per barrel. Under the WTI APO contract multiplier of 1,000 barrels and ordinary minimum price fluctuation of \$0.01 per barrel, that scale corresponds to approximately \$138.40 per contract, or 13.84 ordinary ticks \cite{cme_wti_apo_rule}. This conversion gives the synthetic separation an explicit economic scale; it does not imply that every state attaining the statistical maximum would be equally tradable.

The largest differences occur with short historical samples, high volatility, long times to maturity, few realized fixings, and strikes far from the expected average. For example, with $n=21$, $\sigma_0=0.80$, 126 days to maturity, no realized fixing, and moneyness 1.50, the mean PI--PM gap is 0.1176 and the 99th percentile is 0.1271. With $n=21$, $\sigma_0=0.50$, 252 days, no realized fixing, and moneyness 1.50, the mean gap is 0.1103 and the maximum observed gap is 0.1384.

The approximation in Equation~\eqref{eq:taylor_gap} closely matches the simulated differences. In the most adverse cells, the correlation between the realized PI--PM gap and the second-order approximation is approximately 0.999, with through-origin slopes close to one. Posterior dispersion also behaves as expected: for $\sigma_0=0.80$, mean posterior standard deviation falls from approximately 0.122 at $n=21$ to 0.0159 at $n=1260$. The results show quantitatively that PI and PM can differ substantially when \emph{both} posterior variance and the curvature of the option price with respect to volatility are large.

As more fixings become known, less of the arithmetic average is exposed to future volatility, and the PI--PM difference generally becomes smaller. This pattern appears even when volatility is high and strikes are far from the expected average. The effect can therefore be important early in the averaging period but become small near its end.

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_map} summarizes the experiment. Panel A shows how posterior uncertainty changes with historical sample size. Panels B and C show PI--PM differences across moneyness and realized-fixing fractions. Panel D compares simulated differences with the second-order approximation.

PI and PM are close across these samples, but uncertainty about the model price is much larger than their difference. Across the 310 observations, the mean width of the central 95\% posterior interval for the model price is 0.2733, about 367 times the mean absolute PI--PM difference. For the six observations with positive reported volume, the ratio is about 620. Thus, integrating over the posterior changes the mean price little even though the posterior distribution of model prices is considerably wider.

The sign of the model-to-market errors is equally important. All four historical-volatility rules underprice the option settlements on average. This common bias cannot be explained by choosing PI rather than PM because the two rules produce almost the same prices. The common pricing errors therefore call for a comparison of alternative volatility inputs.

Because the empirical PI--PM differences are sub-cent, Section~\ref{sec:numerical_robustness} subjects them to high-precision pseudo-random Monte Carlo, randomized Sobol, and Curran benchmarks. All three methods reproduce the PI--PM difference with numerical error much smaller than the observed difference. The result is therefore not explained by simulation noise.

\subsection{Historical versus APO-implied effective volatility}

The September--October APO cross-section provides 402 contract-date observations for implied-volatility inversion. All 402 settlements can be mapped to a finite implied volatility under the maintained Curran-equivalent one-factor model. The median historical posterior mean is 0.4155. By contrast, the median date-level common volatility calibrated to the complete APO cross-section is 0.4577, a median difference of 0.0425. Contract-level implied volatilities have a still higher median of 0.4835 because the cross section contains pronounced strike variation.

These implied volatilities do not establish that the structural diffusion coefficients under $\mathbb P$ and $\mathbb Q$ differ. They are values fitted within the maintained pricing model. Their differences from historical volatility are much larger than the PI--PM difference.

Figure~\ref{fig:historical_vs_apo_iv} shows this distinction at both the date and strike levels. The historical posterior mean is relatively stable, whereas APO-implied volatility varies by valuation date and strike.

Leave-one-contract-out calibration provides a first cross-sectional check. Across 399 targets, a common APO-calibrated volatility reduces MAE from 0.3450 for the historical-volatility PI baseline to 0.1557 and RMSE from 0.4625 to 0.1900. The improvement is not uniform, however. The nine positive-volume observations move in the opposite direction in aggregate, and cross-option-type transfer is weaker. These failures are consistent with the fact that one scalar volatility cannot reproduce the observed strike structure and that call/put samples occupy different moneyness regions.

\subsection{Out-of-sample pricing using earlier implied volatility}

We estimate implied-volatility smiles from earlier dates and use them to price APOs on each target date. The experiment contains 2,381 out-of-sample contract-date observations across seven expiries from September 2026 through September 2028. Target-date option settlements are not used to fit the smiles.

The implied volatility required by the maintained pricing model differs substantially across maturities. Median date-level common $\sigma_Q$ is 0.4478, 0.4572, and 0.4444 for the September, October, and November 2026 expiries, respectively; it then declines to 0.3609 for March 2027, 0.3001 for September 2027, 0.2647 for March 2028, and 0.2416 for September 2028. Over the same valuation dates, the historical posterior mean remains near 0.416. These $\sigma_Q$ values are model-equivalent parameters, not structural volatility estimates; the maturity pattern can absorb risk premia, smile effects, omitted futures-curve dynamics, and liquidity or marking effects.

The result is not driven by one expiry. Both prior-date specifications improve MAE and RMSE relative to the historical-volatility baseline in every admitted expiry. In the pooled panel, the expanding smile lowers MAE by 95.8\% and RMSE by 94.9\%, while the previous-day smile lowers MAE by 95.9\% and RMSE by 95.3\%. Mean pricing error moves from +2.4030 under the historical-volatility baseline to $-0.0697$ and $-0.0352$, respectively. The sign change relative to the short-dated October baseline reflects the maturity structure above: a long-window historical volatility near 0.416 is below the effective APO level for some 2026 expiries but substantially above the effective level embedded in the 2027--2028 marks.

We next ask whether more recent historical returns or a dynamic volatility forecast can explain the result. Table~\ref{tab:recent_history_persistence}, Panel A, compares four pre-specified historical-volatility estimates and a horizon-matched Gaussian GARCH(1,1) forecast on the same 2,381 observations. Among the recent-window methods, the 63-return estimate has the lowest pooled error, with MAE 4.5201 and RMSE 5.6465; both exceed the long-window historical PI errors. For GARCH, we estimate parameters from earlier returns, update the conditional variance using the target-date return, and forecast variance for the remaining fixing dates. We then choose one annualized volatility to match the variance of the remaining arithmetic average under the pricing model. GARCH gives MAE 2.6730 and RMSE 3.4441, close to the historical PI values of 2.6187 and 3.4090. None of these tested historical-volatility inputs approaches the option-implied results. This does not rule out other historical forecasting models.

Panel B tests whether fitting an implied-volatility smile is necessary. For each contract with an earlier implied volatility, we carry forward its most recent value and reprice using the valuation-date futures curve, discount factor, and fixing schedule. This is possible for 2,366 observations. The same-contract method has pooled MAE 0.0992 and RMSE 0.1403, compared with 0.1074 and 0.1594 for the fitted previous-day smile. This does not prove that carrying implied volatility forward is generally better. It shows that fitting a smile is not necessary for the large improvement over historical volatility.

Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reconstructs the physical return history contract by contract from official first-nearby CL settlements. On these exact 2,381 holdouts, the reconstructed historical baseline has MAE 2.6468 and RMSE 3.4451 rather than 2.6187 and 3.4090. The option-informed errors are unchanged, so the central forward comparison is insensitive to the continuous-contract return proxy.

Liquidity restrictions preserve the central result. The 62 positive-volume holdouts span 13 valuation-date clusters: historical PI has MAE 1.6340 and RMSE 2.3391, compared with 0.1003 and 0.1358 for the expanding smile and 0.1084 and 0.1333 for the previous-day smile. At open interest of at least 100, the corresponding forward MAEs are approximately 0.106--0.107 versus 1.990 for the historical baseline. However, there are few observations with positive reported volume in some longer-dated expiries. The clearest comparison is therefore for the pooled positive-volume sample and the larger open-interest-filtered samples.

These results indicate that failing to integrate the historical-volatility posterior is not the main explanation for pricing errors in this sample. More recent historical returns and the tested GARCH forecast do not give errors close to those obtained with earlier option-implied volatility. GARCH improves RMSE relative to the long-window historical baseline for October 2026 and September 2028, but remains far from the option-informed specifications at every expiry. The persistence benchmark adds a separate point: on the common sample, carrying a contract's own prior implied volatility yields lower pooled error than the fitted smile, so the historical-versus-option-informed improvement does not depend on cross-sectional smile flexibility.

Parameter uncertainty can still matter: the synthetic experiment identifies cases with substantial PI--PM differences. In the observed out-of-sample comparisons, however, changing the volatility input produces a much larger difference in pricing errors than switching between PI and PM. This remains an empirical benchmark comparison rather than a pure structural decomposition, because option-implied and historical inputs differ in information source and may embed risk premia, omitted dynamics, and market-marking effects.

\subsection{Independent vanilla-WTI validation}\label{sec:external_lo_results}

The APO out-of-sample experiment estimates implied volatility from the same option family that it subsequently prices. The vanilla-WTI experiment addresses that concern by constructing the volatility surface only from prior-date LO and CL settlements. The final strike expansion to 70--120 places 253 of the 280 matched October contract-dates inside the observed prior LO moneyness support, compared with only 60 observations under the initial narrow strike pilot. Only 50 of 620 external-surface prediction rows require any support clipping.

Table~\ref{tab:external_lo} reports the exact no-clipping comparison. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE/RMSE 0.4464/0.5562 with Yahoo \texttt{CL=F} and 0.4362/0.5466 with the reconstructed first-nearby history. The external previous-day LO surface lowers these errors to 0.1559/0.2599, while the expanding LO surface gives 0.1604/0.2858. The corresponding prior-date APO smiles are 0.1676/0.2686 and 0.1612/0.2912. Thus implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options gives a similar improvement over the historical-volatility benchmark. The conclusion is also similar when historical returns are reconstructed from individual futures contracts.

The valuation-date cluster bootstrap confirms that the external improvement over historical volatility is not driven by treating same-day contracts as independent. For the previous-day LO surface, the MAE difference relative to historical PI is $-0.2905$, with a 95\% interval of $[-0.3439,-0.2333]$, and the RMSE difference is $-0.2963$, with interval $[-0.3503,-0.2580]$. The expanding LO surface produces corresponding differences of $-0.2860$ and $-0.2704$, again with intervals entirely below zero.

The external and APO-implied surfaces are much closer to one another. Previous-day LO has an MAE difference of $-0.0117$ relative to the previous-day APO smile, with a 95\% interval of $[-0.0251,-0.0001]$; its RMSE difference is $-0.0087$, with interval $[-0.0149,0.0002]$. For the expanding specifications, both the MAE and RMSE intervals include zero. These results do not establish global superiority of vanilla LO over APO-implied volatility. The main finding is that earlier-date vanilla-WTI implied volatility still produces much lower APO pricing errors than the historical benchmark.

The APO and vanilla-WTI experiments therefore lead to the same conclusion. In this sample, changing from historical volatility to earlier-date option-implied volatility affects pricing errors much more than integrating over the historical-volatility posterior. Bayesian integration accounts for uncertainty conditional on the pricing model; it does not ensure that the chosen volatility input matches market settlements.
