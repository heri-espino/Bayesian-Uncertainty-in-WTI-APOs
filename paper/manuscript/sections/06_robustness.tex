\section{Robustness and limitations}\label{sec:robustness}

We summarize the robustness evidence relevant to the central comparisons. The online supplement contains full sampler settings, alternative return-window results, posterior calibration, numerical tests, LO tree refinement, and liquidity tables.

\subsection{Historical-model and data robustness}

The historical-versus-option-implied comparison is robust across the tested historical-volatility specifications. Table~\ref{tab:recent_history_persistence} shows that the evaluated rolling-window and EWMA rules do not reproduce the prior option-implied performance, while the horizon-matched GARCH benchmark remains close to the long-window historical baseline. GARCH allows conditional heteroskedasticity and forecasts variance over the remaining fixing horizon, but its pooled pricing errors remain substantially above those of earlier option-implied volatility. This result applies only to the evaluated specifications.

Alternative physical-return assumptions also leave the main comparison largely unchanged. Standardized Student-\(t\) innovations modestly improve historical-volatility pricing without materially increasing the PI--PM difference relative to pricing errors. Reconstructing returns from individual first-nearby CL contracts gives a posterior close to the Yahoo \texttt{CL=F} baseline. The out-of-sample comparison changes little. Prior and estimation-window sensitivity show that, once the long historical likelihood is informative, reasonable prior changes have little effect; shorter windows matter more because they sample different volatility regimes.

Posterior-mean and posterior-RMS volatility yield similar errors when both are used as constant inputs to Curran's approximation. This sensitivity does not replace posterior integration.

\subsection{Numerical and statistical validation}\label{sec:numerical_robustness}

Numerical precision matters because the empirical PI--PM difference is sub-cent. High-precision pseudo-random Monte Carlo, independently scrambled Sobol calculations, and Curran conditioning agree on the PI--PM difference to a scale well below the observed empirical gaps. The small October PI--PM difference is therefore not explained by simulation noise or the choice of numerical pricing method.

Simulation-based calibration provides a separate check on posterior inference. Prior-predictive rank distributions and interval coverage are close to their nominal targets across the tested sample sizes. For the external LO inversion, comparing 80-, 160-, and 320-step Cox--Ross--Rubinstein trees shows that the LO inversion is stable around the 160-step tree used in the main analysis, while a separate surface-shape audit finds finite positive fitted volatilities and smooth behavior inside observed prior support. These tests support numerical stability. They do not prove exact tree convergence or that the fitted volatility surface is arbitrage-free everywhere.

\subsection{Liquidity, persistence, and external transfer}

We use a valuation-date cluster bootstrap to preserve contemporaneous cross-contract dependence. Valuation-date cluster bootstrap results confirm lower pricing errors with earlier option-implied volatility. The same pattern appears when observations are restricted by positive reported volume or open interest, although few contracts have positive reported volume in the longest-dated expiries. The empirical claims therefore concern available end-of-day market marks rather than a dense transaction-price panel.

The same-contract persistence benchmark gives lower pooled errors than the fitted previous-day smile on common observations. While not a general ranking of these methods, it shows that the option-implied advantage does not depend on fitting a cross-sectional smile.

The LO comparison is insensitive to the scalar-volatility mapping: fixing-count weighted RMS and variance matching for the remaining arithmetic average yield similar pricing errors. Neither rule is asserted to be an exact structural representation.

\subsection{Limits of interpretation}

Although the main APO panel spans seven expiries, it contains only 15 target dates; the external sample contains 10. Cross-sectional breadth should not be mistaken for independent time-series information. Date-cluster resampling preserves contemporaneous dependence but does not remove serial dependence across adjacent dates.

The maintained one-factor, constant-volatility specification excludes multifactor futures-curve dynamics, stochastic volatility, jumps, and stochastic convenience yields. Such extensions may improve pricing fit but introduce additional uncertain parameters. The present results do not identify a structural difference between volatility under \(\mathbb P\) and \(\mathbb Q\). Implied volatility can reflect risk premia, omitted dynamics, liquidity, and settlement conventions. Our conclusions are limited to the chosen model and the volatility inputs tested.
