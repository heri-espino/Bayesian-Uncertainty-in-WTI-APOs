\section{Robustness and limitations}\label{sec:robustness}

We summarize the main checks here. The online supplement contains full sampler settings, results for other return windows, posterior calibration, numerical comparisons, tree-size checks, and liquidity tables.

\subsection{Historical-model and data robustness}

The result is not specific to one historical-volatility estimate. Table~\ref{tab:recent_history_persistence} shows that the evaluated rolling-window and EWMA rules do not reproduce the prior option-implied performance, while the horizon-matched GARCH benchmark remains close to the long-window historical baseline. GARCH allows volatility to vary over time and provides variance forecasts for the remaining fixing dates. Yet its pooled pricing errors are still much higher than those from earlier option-implied volatility. This conclusion concerns only the methods tested.

Other changes to the return model have little effect on the comparison. Standardized Student-\(t\) returns improve historical-volatility pricing modestly, but PI and PM remain close relative to the model's pricing error. Reconstructing returns from individual first-nearby CL contracts gives a posterior close to the Yahoo \texttt{CL=F} baseline. The out-of-sample comparison changes little. Prior and estimation-window sensitivity show that, once the long historical likelihood is informative, reasonable prior changes have little effect; shorter windows matter more because they sample different volatility regimes.

We compare posterior mean volatility with posterior RMS volatility using Curran's approximation in both cases. Their pooled pricing errors are close. This is a comparison of two constant-volatility inputs, not a test of posterior integration.

\subsection{Numerical and statistical validation}\label{sec:numerical_robustness}

Because PI and PM differ so little, numerical accuracy is important. High-precision pseudo-random Monte Carlo, independently scrambled Sobol calculations, and Curran conditioning agree on the PI--PM difference to a scale well below the observed empirical gaps. The small October PI--PM difference is therefore not explained by simulation noise or the choice of numerical pricing method.

We check the posterior calculation separately using simulation-based calibration. Prior-predictive rank distributions and interval coverage are close to their nominal targets across the tested sample sizes. For the external LO inversion, comparing 80-, 160-, and 320-step Cox--Ross--Rubinstein trees shows that the LO inversion is stable around the 160-step tree used in the main analysis, while a separate surface-shape audit finds finite positive fitted volatilities and smooth behavior inside observed prior support. These tests support numerical stability. They do not prove exact tree convergence or that the fitted volatility surface is arbitrage-free everywhere.

\subsection{Liquidity, persistence, and external transfer}

We account for dependence among contracts by resampling valuation dates, not individual contracts. Valuation-date cluster bootstrap results confirm lower pricing errors with earlier option-implied volatility. The same pattern appears when observations are restricted by positive reported volume or open interest, although few contracts have positive reported volume in the longest-dated expiries. The empirical claims therefore concern available end-of-day market marks rather than a dense transaction-price panel.

We also check whether an implied-volatility smile is needed. On the common sample, carrying forward each contract's latest earlier implied volatility gives lower pooled errors than fitting a previous-day smile. This is not evidence that carrying implied volatility forward will always work better. It shows that the improvement over historical volatility does not require smile fitting.

The LO result changes little under another rule for combining volatility estimates across maturities. We compare the main fixing-count weighted RMS rule with a volatility that matches the variance of the remaining average. Both produce similar pricing errors; the RMS rule need not be an exact variance identity.

\subsection{Limits of interpretation}

The main APO comparison covers seven expiries but only 15 target dates. The external LO comparison covers 10 dates. Many contracts on one date do not provide many independent market histories. Date-cluster resampling preserves contemporaneous dependence but does not remove serial dependence across adjacent dates.

The model is deliberately simple. Several futures-curve factors, stochastic volatility, jumps, convenience yields, or more detailed interest-rate modeling could improve pricing fit, but would add uncertain parameters. The results do not identify a structural difference between volatility under \(\mathbb P\) and \(\mathbb Q\). Implied volatility can reflect risk premia, omitted dynamics, liquidity, and settlement conventions. Our conclusions are limited to the chosen model and the volatility inputs tested.
