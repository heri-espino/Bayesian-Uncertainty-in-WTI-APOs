\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Option prices depend on estimates of volatility and other parameters. Bayesian pricing accounts for this uncertainty by averaging option values over the parameter posterior instead of using a single estimate. The approach is established in option pricing: Bayesian learning has been used in implied-volatility forecasting \cite{guidolin2003}, risk-neutral predictive densities \cite{romboutsstentoft2014}, and calibration of exotic-derivative models \cite{guptareisinger2014}. We ask when this averaging changes the price substantially compared with using posterior mean volatility.

Average-price commodity options are useful here. Their sensitivity to volatility depends on the strike, time to maturity, and number of prices already fixed. The numerical literature exploits geometric-average structure to price arithmetic-average claims efficiently \cite{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}, while commodity-specific models allow richer futures dynamics \cite{schwartz1997,ewaldwu2023}. For WTI specifically, \cite{shirayatakahashi2011} calibrate richer risk-neutral models to liquid vanilla options before valuing average-price options, and \cite{ganwangyang2020} study WTI Average Price Options with a model-free machine-learning approach. We do not introduce a new pricing model. We keep one simple model and compare two decisions: whether to average over the volatility posterior and whether to use historical or option-implied volatility.

We estimate volatility from historical returns under the physical measure, but value options under the risk-neutral measure. We keep these two steps separate. Let \(C^Q(\sigma)\) denote the risk-neutral APO value conditional on volatility \(\sigma\). The posterior-integrated and posterior-mean rules are
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],
 \qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D]).
 \label{eq:intro_rules}
\end{equation}
Because option value need not depend linearly on volatility, PI and PM may differ. A second-order expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}
 \approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D),
 \qquad
 \bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D].
 \label{eq:intro_taylor}
\end{equation}
The size and sign of the difference depend on posterior variance and price curvature. Uncertainty about the model price follows a different relationship: its standard deviation is locally proportional to the posterior standard deviation of volatility. PI and PM can therefore be close even when volatility uncertainty produces a wide range of model prices.

The empirical application uses CME WTI Average Price Options. Their payoff depends on the average of daily first-nearby WTI futures settlements. We account for known fixings, future fixing dates, and the futures curve observed when each option is priced. We then conduct three comparisons. First, a synthetic experiment identifies when the PI--PM difference is large. Second, an out-of-sample APO pricing experiment compares historical-volatility inputs with implied volatility estimated from earlier option observations. Third, a cross-instrument check estimates implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options rather than from APOs.

Option-implied volatility may help explain market prices even when the pricing model is imperfect. Forward-looking crude-oil option information has been shown to contain predictive volatility content beyond historical models \cite{gildertsiaras2020}, and flexible implied-volatility specifications can fit the contemporaneous cross-section while still deteriorating out of sample \cite{dumasflemingwhaley1998}. More generally, richer option models can improve some performance criteria without dominating on all dimensions \cite{bakshicaochen1997,cumminsesposito2025}. We use implied volatility as an input to the chosen pricing model, not as a uniquely identified risk-neutral diffusion parameter.

We find three main results. First, the synthetic experiment supports Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_taylor}: PI and PM can differ substantially when the return history is short and price curvature is strong. Second, they are nearly identical in the observed WTI sample, even though parameter uncertainty produces a much wider range of model prices. Third, volatility estimated from earlier option prices leads to much lower subsequent pricing errors than the historical methods tested. Shorter historical windows, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH do not achieve similar errors. This improvement does not require fitting a flexible implied-volatility smile and persists when implied volatility comes from standard monthly WTI futures options.

These comparisons do not measure the structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Implied volatility may also reflect risk premia, dynamics missing from the model, liquidity, and settlement practices. The results apply to the sample and methods studied.
