\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Option prices depend on parameters that are estimated rather than observed. Bayesian pricing can account for parameter uncertainty by averaging conditional option values over the parameter posterior instead of using only point estimates. This idea is established in option pricing: Bayesian learning has been used in implied-volatility forecasting \cite{guidolin2003}, risk-neutral predictive densities \cite{romboutsstentoft2014}, and calibration of exotic-derivative models \cite{guptareisinger2014}. Here we ask when that averaging materially changes a price relative to using posterior mean volatility.

Average-price commodity options are useful for this comparison because the sensitivity of their prices to volatility varies with moneyness, maturity, and how much of the averaging period has already passed. The numerical literature exploits geometric-average structure to price arithmetic-average claims efficiently \cite{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}, while commodity-specific models allow richer futures dynamics \cite{schwartz1997,ewaldwu2023}. For WTI specifically, \cite{shirayatakahashi2011} calibrate richer risk-neutral models to liquid vanilla options before valuing average-price options, and \cite{ganwangyang2020} study WTI Average Price Options with a model-free machine-learning approach. We do not introduce a new APO pricing model. Instead, we hold a simple pricing model fixed and compare the effect of integrating over the volatility posterior with the effect of changing the volatility input.

The statistical and pricing measures are kept separate. Historical returns inform volatility under the physical measure, while option valuation is performed under the risk-neutral measure. Let \(C^Q(\sigma)\) denote the risk-neutral APO value conditional on volatility \(\sigma\). The posterior-integrated and posterior-mean rules are
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],
 \qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D]).
 \label{eq:intro_rules}
\end{equation}
Because \(C^Q(\sigma)\) is nonlinear, these quantities need not coincide. A second-order expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}
 \approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D),
 \qquad
 \bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D].
 \label{eq:intro_taylor}
\end{equation}
The PI--PM difference is larger when the volatility posterior is more dispersed and the option price is more curved as a function of volatility. By contrast, the spread of the posterior distribution of model prices is locally proportional to the posterior standard deviation. PI and PM can therefore be close even when uncertainty about the model price is substantial.

The empirical application uses CME WTI Average Price Options. The payoff depends on the arithmetic average of daily first-nearby WTI futures settlements, so valuation reconstructs realized fixings, the remaining first-nearby futures sequence, and the contemporaneous futures curve. We conduct three comparisons. First, a synthetic experiment identifies when the PI--PM difference is large. Second, an out-of-sample APO pricing experiment compares historical-volatility inputs with implied volatility estimated from earlier option observations. Third, a cross-instrument check estimates implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options rather than from APOs.

The empirical distinction between integration and specification is important because option-implied information can outperform historical return models even when the pricing model remains imperfect. Forward-looking crude-oil option information has been shown to contain predictive volatility content beyond historical models \cite{gildertsiaras2020}, and flexible implied-volatility specifications can fit the contemporaneous cross-section while still deteriorating out of sample \cite{dumasflemingwhaley1998}. More generally, richer option models can improve some performance criteria without dominating on all dimensions \cite{bakshicaochen1997,cumminsesposito2025}. We therefore treat implied volatility as an input to the maintained pricing model, not as an estimate of a unique structural risk-neutral diffusion coefficient.

The results have three parts. First, the synthetic experiment supports Equation~\eqref{eq:intro_taylor}: the PI--PM difference can be large with short historical samples, high volatility, long maturities, and strong price curvature. Second, PI and PM are almost identical in the observed WTI sample, although the posterior distribution of model prices remains much wider than their difference. Third, implied volatility estimated from earlier option observations produces much lower subsequent pricing errors than the historical-volatility benchmarks tested. Shorter historical windows, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH do not achieve similar errors. This improvement does not require fitting a flexible implied-volatility smile and persists when implied volatility comes from standard monthly WTI futures options.

These comparisons do not measure a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Under a simple pricing model, implied volatility can reflect risk premia, omitted dynamics, liquidity, and settlement conventions. Our conclusion is limited to the sample and methods studied: earlier-date option-implied volatility gives lower subsequent APO pricing errors than the historical-volatility inputs tested, while integration over the historical-volatility posterior changes the mean model price very little.
