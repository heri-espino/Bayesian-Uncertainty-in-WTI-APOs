\section{Results}\label{sec:short_results}

\subsection{When does posterior integration matter?}

Figure~\ref{fig:short_mechanism} shows when posterior integration affects point prices in the simulated settings. The largest differences occur with short estimation windows, high volatility, long maturities, few realized fixings, and pronounced price curvature. Across the 10 million synthetic histories, the maximum observed absolute difference is 0.1565 for a normalized \$100 forward, and the largest within-cell 99th percentile is 0.1273. These extremes exceed the differences observed in the October-2026 sample. The sample maximum depends on the number of simulated histories; the within-cell percentile is less sensitive to rare simulated outcomes.

The second-order approximation tracks the simulated differences closely: the correlation between cell-level mean PI--PM differences and the curvature--variance term is 0.9995. Through-origin slopes are also close to one in the most extreme settings. These results support posterior variance and price curvature as the principal local determinants of the difference.

The PI--PM difference generally declines as the share of realized fixings increases. The relationship is not strictly monotone: in Panel C, the 252-day case initially rises because partial fixing changes both the remaining volatility exposure and local price curvature. The difference nevertheless tends toward zero as the averaging period ends.

\subsection{A bounded check of estimation and model error}

The independent-seed synthetic follow-up has 160 return histories and
480 option-state rows at each reference correlation. Under the maintained
one-factor payoff model ($\rho=1$), the mean absolute PI--PM gap is
\$0.003709 per barrel, whereas the mean plug-in error against the independently
simulated, known-volatility payoff reference is \$0.318736 per barrel.
At the illustrative \$0.005-per-barrel cutoff, 327 of 480 option states
have an absolute PI--PM gap at or below the cutoff; 315 of these still have
plug-in errors above it. This conditional 96.3\% proportion is specific to
the tight threshold, synthetic design, and reused histories, not a market
risk probability. It tests whether small estimator disagreement implies
small total pricing error, not whether the second-order Taylor approximation
predicts the PI--PM gap.

Table~\ref{tab:short_controlled_stress} reports the same posterior under
three hypothetical futures-correlation structures. The measured PI--PM
gap does not change because the posterior pricing model stays at $\rho=1$.
Relative to each separately simulated fixed-volatility payoff reference,
plug-in errors increase and nominal 95\% posterior model-price interval
coverage falls as the cross-contract correlation weight decreases.
These rates are provisional: they describe sensitivity within a stylized
model, not empirical coverage or an estimate of WTI correlations.

\subsection{Posterior integration in the WTI sample}

The October-2026 sample contains 310 contract-date observations across 12 valuation dates. Table~\ref{tab:short_october} shows nearly identical pricing errors for PI and PM (RMSE 0.5177 and 0.5183, respectively). Their mean absolute price difference is only 0.000745 dollars per barrel.

The central 95\% posterior interval for conditional model prices has mean width 0.2733 dollars per barrel, approximately 367 times the mean absolute PI--PM difference. This shows why a small integration correction does not imply a narrow posterior distribution of model prices; it does not establish the economic value of that uncertainty. Both methods underprice October settlements on average.

\subsection{Historical versus option-implied volatility}

Table~\ref{tab:short_forward} reports conditional pricing errors for 2,381 contract-date observations across 15 valuation dates. The historical PI benchmark has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090, compared with 0.1075 and 0.1594 for the previous-day APO implied-volatility smile. The expanding earlier-date smile performs similarly. Both specifications reduce pricing errors for all seven expiries, with valuation-date cluster bootstrap intervals for the error differences below zero in each expiry (Figure~\ref{fig:short_forward_bootstrap}).

Rolling-window, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) benchmarks do not close the pricing-error gap (Online Supplement Section S1 and Table S1). In particular, GARCH gives MAE 2.6730 and RMSE 3.4441 on the same observations.

Panel B isolates 2,366 observations with an earlier implied volatility for the same contract. Carrying that volatility forward yields lower pooled errors than the fitted previous-day smile (MAE 0.0992 versus 0.1074). This benchmark shows that the advantage over historical volatility does not require smile fitting; it does not establish a general ranking of persistence and fitted-smile methods.

\subsection{Cross-instrument validation using vanilla-WTI options}

The cross-instrument comparison replaces APO-implied volatility with estimates from standard monthly WTI futures options. Table~\ref{tab:short_lo} uses 253 October-2026 APO contract-date observations across 10 valuation dates. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. A previous-day vanilla-WTI implied-volatility specification based on LO options lowers these errors to 0.1559 and 0.2599. The corresponding previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1676 and RMSE 0.2686.

Valuation-date cluster bootstrap results support the improvement over historical volatility. Differences between LO-implied and APO-implied specifications are small and do not establish consistent superiority by MAE and RMSE. The cross-instrument test instead shows that the advantage persists when implied volatility comes from another WTI option family.
