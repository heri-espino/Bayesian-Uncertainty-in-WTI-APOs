\section{Results}\label{sec:short_results}

\subsection{When does posterior integration matter?}

Figure~\ref{fig:short_mechanism} shows how the PI--PM difference varies across the synthetic design. The largest differences occur with short estimation windows, high volatility, long maturities, few realized fixings, and pronounced price curvature. The maximum absolute difference is 0.1384 for a normalized \$100 forward, and the largest within-cell 99th percentile is 0.1271. Both exceed the differences observed in the October-2026 sample.

The second-order approximation tracks the simulated differences closely: the correlation between cell-level mean PI--PM differences and the curvature--variance term is 0.9995. Through-origin slopes are also close to one in the most extreme settings. These results support posterior variance and price curvature as the principal local determinants of the difference.

The PI--PM difference generally declines as the share of realized fixings increases. The relationship is not strictly monotone: in Panel C, the 252-day case initially rises because partial fixing changes both the remaining volatility exposure and local price curvature. The difference nevertheless tends toward zero as the averaging period ends.

\subsection{WTI point-price correction versus parameter uncertainty}

The October-2026 baseline contains 310 contract-date observations over 12 eligible valuation dates. Table~\ref{tab:short_october} reports the main Bayesian comparison. Posterior-integrated and posterior-mean plug-in prices have nearly identical market-pricing errors: RMSE is 0.5177 for PI and 0.5183 for PM. The mean absolute difference between the two prices is only 0.000745.

The small PI--PM difference contrasts with the dispersion of conditional model prices. The mean width of the central 95\% posterior interval is 0.2733, approximately 367 times the mean absolute PI--PM difference. The former describes parameter-induced variation in model values; the latter measures the effect of integration on the point estimate.

Both methods underprice October settlements on average, while their mean absolute price difference is less than one tenth of a cent per barrel. Posterior integration therefore does not account for the common pricing bias.

\subsection{Historical versus option-implied volatility}

Table~\ref{tab:short_forward} reports the central out-of-sample comparison. On the same 2,381 contract-date observations, the long-window historical posterior-integrated benchmark has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090. The best of the pre-specified rolling-window comparisons, based on the most recent 63 returns, has still larger errors, with MAE 4.5201 and RMSE 5.6465. The horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) benchmark produces MAE 2.6730 and RMSE 3.4441, close to the long-window historical benchmark.

By contrast, the previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1075 and RMSE 0.1594. The expanding earlier-date smile produces similarly small errors, 0.1088 and 0.1725. Earlier-date implied volatility gives lower errors for all seven expiries. Bootstrap intervals based on valuation-date clusters also lie below zero for each expiry (Figure~\ref{fig:short_forward_bootstrap}).

Neither shorter return windows nor EWMA approaches the option-implied performance (Online Supplement Section S1 and Table S1). Horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) also remains close to the long-window historical benchmark, indicating that these particular time-varying volatility estimates do not close the pricing-error gap.

Panel B evaluates same-contract implied-volatility persistence. On 2,366 common observations, carrying forward the latest earlier implied volatility gives lower pooled MAE and RMSE than the fitted previous-day smile. The comparison does not establish a general ranking of these methods, but shows that the option-implied advantage does not require cross-sectional smile fitting.

The lower pricing errors reflect differences in information and possibly model misspecification, not an identified structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Implied volatility can also absorb risk premia, omitted dynamics, liquidity effects, and settlement conventions.

\subsection{Cross-instrument validation using vanilla-WTI options}

The cross-instrument comparison replaces APO-implied volatility with estimates from standard monthly WTI futures options. Table~\ref{tab:short_lo} uses 253 October-2026 APO contract-date observations across 10 valuation dates. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. A previous-day vanilla-WTI implied-volatility specification based on LO options lowers these errors to 0.1559 and 0.2599. The corresponding previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1676 and RMSE 0.2686.

Valuation-date cluster bootstrap results support the improvement over historical volatility. Differences between LO-implied and APO-implied specifications are small and do not establish consistent superiority by MAE and RMSE. The cross-instrument test instead shows that the advantage persists when implied volatility comes from another WTI option family.

Taken together, the results distinguish the potential effect of posterior integration from its small empirical effect in this sample. The choice of volatility input has much larger consequences for out-of-sample pricing errors.
