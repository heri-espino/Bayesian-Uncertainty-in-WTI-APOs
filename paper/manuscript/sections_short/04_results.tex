\section{Results}\label{sec:short_results}

\subsection{When does posterior integration matter?}

Figure~\ref{fig:short_mechanism} summarizes the synthetic mechanism experiment. PI and PM tend to differ more when the return history is short, volatility is high, maturity is long, or few fixings are known. Strong price curvature also increases the difference. Across the full synthetic experiment, the maximum absolute PI--PM difference is 0.1384 on a normalized \$100 forward, and the largest cell-level 99th percentile is 0.1271. These differences are much larger than those in the October-2026 WTI sample.

The second-order approximation in Equation~\eqref{eq:short_taylor} closely tracks the simulated PI--PM difference. Across synthetic cells, the correlation between the cell-level mean gap and the curvature--variance approximation is 0.9995. In the most adverse cells, through-origin slopes are close to one. Large differences occur when uncertainty about volatility and price curvature are both high.

As more fixings become known, the PI--PM difference generally shrinks because less of the average remains exposed to future volatility. It does not fall at every step. In Panel C, the 252-day case first rises and then falls. As fixings become known, both the exposure to future volatility and price curvature change. Overall, the difference tends to shrink near the end of the averaging period.

\subsection{WTI point-price correction versus parameter uncertainty}

The October-2026 baseline contains 310 contract-date observations over 12 eligible valuation dates. Table~\ref{tab:short_october} reports the main Bayesian comparison. Posterior-integrated and posterior-mean plug-in prices have nearly identical market-pricing errors: RMSE is 0.5177 for PI and 0.5183 for PM. The mean absolute difference between the two prices is only 0.000745.

Yet volatility uncertainty still produces a range of model prices. The mean width of the central 95\% posterior price interval is 0.2733, about 367 times the mean absolute PI--PM difference. PI--PM measures the change in the point price from posterior averaging. The interval measures how much the model price varies across the volatility posterior.

Both PI and PM underprice settlements on average in the October sample. Their prices differ by less than one tenth of a cent per barrel on average. The pricing errors are therefore not mainly due to the choice between PI and PM.

\subsection{Historical versus option-implied volatility}

Table~\ref{tab:short_forward} reports the central out-of-sample comparison. On the same 2,381 contract-date observations, the long-window historical posterior-integrated benchmark has MAE 2.6187 and RMSE 3.4090. The best of the pre-specified rolling-window comparisons, based on the most recent 63 returns, has still larger errors, with MAE 4.5201 and RMSE 5.6465. The horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) benchmark produces MAE 2.6730 and RMSE 3.4441, close to the long-window historical benchmark.

By contrast, the previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1075 and RMSE 0.1594. The expanding earlier-date smile produces similarly small errors, 0.1088 and 0.1725. Earlier-date implied volatility gives lower errors for all seven expiries. Bootstrap intervals based on valuation-date clusters also lie below zero for each expiry (Figure~\ref{fig:short_forward_bootstrap}).

Using more recent historical returns does not produce similar errors. Shorter return windows and an exponentially weighted estimate also perform worse (Online Supplement Section S1 and Table S1). Allowing volatility to vary over time also does not close the gap in the tested GARCH(1,1) model, whose pooled errors remain close to those of the long-window historical benchmark.

Panel B tests whether fitting a smile is needed. For 2,366 observations, carrying forward each contract's latest implied volatility gives lower pooled MAE and RMSE than using the fitted previous-day smile. This does not show that carrying volatility forward will always work better. It shows that smile fitting is not required to outperform the historical benchmarks in this sample.

These are pricing comparisons within the chosen model. Historical and implied volatility use different information. Implied volatility may reflect risk premia, omitted price dynamics, or how market settlements are determined. Lower pricing errors do not establish how much physical and risk-neutral volatility differ.

\subsection{Cross-instrument validation using vanilla-WTI options}

The independent vanilla-WTI experiment moves the implied-volatility source outside the APO market. Table~\ref{tab:short_lo} reports the exact common sample of 253 October-2026 APO contract-dates over 10 valuation dates. Historical-volatility posterior integration has MAE 0.4464 and RMSE 0.5562. A previous-day vanilla-WTI implied-volatility specification based on LO options lowers these errors to 0.1559 and 0.2599. The corresponding previous-day APO implied-volatility smile has MAE 0.1676 and RMSE 0.2686.

The cluster bootstrap also supports the lower errors from LO-implied volatility relative to historical volatility. LO and APO implied-volatility results are close, with no consistent advantage across MAE and RMSE. The important result is that earlier option-implied volatility still helps when it comes from a different WTI option market.

Overall, the synthetic experiment shows when posterior averaging can matter. In the observed WTI sample, PI and PM are close, while using earlier option-implied volatility reduces pricing errors much more.
