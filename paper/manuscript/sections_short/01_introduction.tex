\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

Parameter uncertainty enters option pricing when model parameters are estimated rather than observed. Bayesian valuation can account for this uncertainty by integrating conditional option values over the parameter posterior instead of substituting a point estimate \citep{guidolin2003,romboutsstentoft2014,guptareisinger2014}. We examine when posterior integration materially changes the value of a WTI Average Price Option (APO).

The volatility sensitivity of an arithmetic-average Asian option depends on moneyness, maturity, and the fraction of the averaging period already fixed. Geometric-average methods and conditioning approximations provide established numerical approaches \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. Commodity applications must also account for the futures term structure; richer specifications allow for stochastic convenience yields, jumps, or stochastic volatility \citep{schwartz1997,shirayatakahashi2011,ewaldwu2023}. Rather than proposing another pricing model, we hold the one-factor specification fixed and examine both posterior integration and the choice of volatility input.

These comparisons isolate different questions. Posterior integration concerns how parameter uncertainty enters a conditional pricing function. The historical-versus-implied comparison concerns the volatility information used for valuation.

Let \(C^Q(\sigma)\) be the risk-neutral option value conditional on volatility \(\sigma\), let \(\mathcal D\) denote the historical return data, and let \(\bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D]\). The posterior-integrated price and posterior-mean plug-in price are
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],
 \qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\bar\sigma).
 \label{eq:short_pi_pm}
\end{equation}
A second-order expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}
 \approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D).
 \label{eq:short_taylor}
\end{equation}
The approximation shows that the PI--PM difference depends locally on posterior variance and the curvature of the pricing function with respect to volatility. By contrast, the posterior standard deviation of model prices is first order in the posterior standard deviation of volatility. A negligible PI--PM difference therefore need not imply negligible parameter-induced price uncertainty.

The empirical analysis uses CME WTI APOs, which settle against the calendar-month average of first-nearby WTI futures prices. We compare historical-volatility estimates with implied volatility obtained from APOs and standard monthly WTI futures options. Implied-volatility estimates use only earlier option observations; each target contract is repriced using the valuation-date futures curve, realized fixings, and discount factor. Historical estimates follow their stated return-data cutoff.

Three results emerge. First, the synthetic experiment supports Equation~\eqref{eq:short_taylor} and identifies large PI--PM differences in short-sample, high-curvature settings. Second, PI and PM are nearly identical for October-2026 WTI APOs, although the posterior distribution of model prices is considerably wider. Third, earlier-date option-implied volatility produces substantially lower out-of-sample pricing errors than the tested rolling-window, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) benchmarks. This advantage does not depend on fitting a volatility smile and also appears when implied volatility is estimated from standard monthly WTI futures options.

These are pricing comparisons under a maintained model, not estimates of a structural wedge between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Implied volatility may incorporate risk premia, omitted dynamics, liquidity effects, and settlement conventions. We restrict our conclusions to the samples and specifications evaluated.
