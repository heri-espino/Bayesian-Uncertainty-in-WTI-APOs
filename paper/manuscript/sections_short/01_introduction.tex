\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

Uncertainty in estimated volatility can leave an option's point value almost unchanged while producing a dispersed distribution of conditional option values. For a pricing exercise, these are different consequences of the same estimation problem. Their importance also depends on the volatility information supplied to the model: integrating a historical-volatility posterior may have little effect on a point price even when an option-implied volatility input changes that price substantially. This paper measures these three quantities together for WTI Average Price Options (APOs).

Bayesian option pricing already provides methods for integrating parameter uncertainty and constructing predictive distributions \citep{guidolin2003,romboutsstentoft2014,guptareisinger2014}. A particularly close antecedent is \citet{romboutsstentoft2014}, who find similar classical and Bayesian option-pricing errors with abundant return data and identify potential scope for Bayesian methods with smaller samples. Their result motivates a more specific question: under a fixed posterior and pricing model, which combinations of information and contract state make integration consequential, and how large is that correction relative to posterior price dispersion and to changing the volatility input?

Arithmetic-average options make contract state central to that question. Moneyness, remaining horizon, and the fraction of the average already fixed affect the pricing function's sensitivity and curvature with respect to volatility. Efficient valuation methods exploit the relationship between arithmetic and geometric averages \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. Commodity applications also require contract-specific futures maturities and may incorporate mean reversion, convenience yields, jumps, or stochastic volatility \citep{schwartz1997,ewaldwu2023}. For WTI average options, \citet{shirayatakahashi2011} already calibrate richer models to liquid American vanilla futures options before valuing the average payoff. The use of vanilla option information is therefore an established calibration principle; here it provides a cross-instrument check on the relative importance of volatility information and posterior integration.

Let $C^Q(\sigma)$ denote the conditional risk-neutral value at volatility $\sigma$, let $\mathcal D$ denote historical return data, and set $\bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D]$. The posterior-integrated price (PI) and posterior-mean plug-in price (PM) are
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],\qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\bar\sigma).
 \label{eq:short_pi_pm}
\end{equation}
Their difference isolates the effect of integrating the same volatility posterior rather than evaluating the same pricing function at its mean. Locally,
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}\approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D).
 \label{eq:short_taylor}
\end{equation}
The correction depends on both posterior dispersion and volatility curvature. Posterior price dispersion instead depends locally on volatility sensitivity times posterior standard deviation. A small PI--PM correction can therefore coexist with appreciable uncertainty in the conditional option value.

The contribution is a quantitative assessment of this distinction across synthetic contract states and observed WTI settlements. The synthetic experiment maps the integration correction over information levels, volatility, moneyness, horizon, and partial fixing. The October-2026 application places the correction beside posterior model-price intervals. Later-date valuation exercises then compare the historical-volatility baseline with recent historical estimators, horizon-matched GARCH forecasts, prior APO implied volatility, and prior vanilla-WTI implied volatility. A same-contract lagged-IV comparator tests whether the option-implied improvement requires a fitted cross-sectional smile.

The findings have a common interpretation. Integration becomes appreciable when posterior dispersion and price curvature are jointly large. In the observed October sample, PI and PM are almost identical despite wider posterior model-price intervals. Across the later-date valuations, the evaluated option-implied inputs improve settlement fit much more than integrating the historical posterior. This is evidence about valuation conditional on current market state and a specified model. It does not establish a structural physical-versus-risk-neutral volatility decomposition or an ex ante trading forecast.
