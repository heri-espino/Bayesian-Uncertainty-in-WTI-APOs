\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

Bayesian option valuation can account for parameter uncertainty by integrating conditional prices over a posterior distribution rather than using a point estimate \citep{guidolin2003,romboutsstentoft2014,guptareisinger2014}. Whether this integration materially changes the option price depends on the pricing function and the dispersion of the parameter posterior. We study this question for WTI Average Price Options (APOs), focusing on uncertainty about volatility estimated from historical returns.

Arithmetic-average Asian option values are sensitive to moneyness, maturity, and the proportion of the average already fixed. Established methods exploit geometric-average control variates or conditioning approximations \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. Commodity applications must also account for the futures term structure; richer models can incorporate stochastic convenience yields, jumps, and stochastic volatility \citep{schwartz1997,shirayatakahashi2011,ewaldwu2023}. We instead hold a one-factor pricing model fixed to distinguish two effects: posterior integration of historical-volatility uncertainty and the choice between historical and option-implied volatility inputs.

These effects address different questions. The posterior-integrated price (PI) averages conditional option values over the volatility posterior, whereas the posterior-mean plug-in price (PM) uses posterior mean volatility. Their difference measures the effect of integration on the point price, not the full dispersion of model prices caused by parameter uncertainty. The volatility-input comparison asks a separate empirical question: which estimates give smaller errors against settlement-price proxies under the same pricing model? Because repricing uses the valuation-date futures curve and already known fixings, the exercise is not a fully prospective trading or price forecast.

We investigate the PI--PM difference with 10 million synthetic return histories and October-2026 WTI APO observations. We then compare historical-volatility benchmarks with implied volatility estimated from earlier APO dates in a date-ordered conditional pricing panel with 15 valuation dates. As a cross-instrument check, we obtain implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options instead of APOs. Implied-volatility estimates use only earlier option observations; repricing uses the valuation-date futures curve, realized fixings, and discount factor.

The synthetic results show that posterior integration can matter when historical samples are short and the pricing function has substantial curvature in volatility. In the observed October-2026 sample, PI and PM are nearly identical despite much wider posterior dispersion in model prices. Earlier-date option-implied volatility also yields lower out-of-sample pricing errors than the tested historical benchmarks, including rolling windows, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH(1,1). This advantage persists without fitting a volatility smile and in the cross-instrument check.

This differs from model-complexity comparisons involving alternative stochastic-volatility and jump specifications, such as \citet{cumminsesposito2025}. We hold the pricing model fixed and examine the consequences of volatility estimation, posterior averaging, and parameter-induced uncertainty for an arithmetic-average commodity option. These are conditional comparisons within a maintained pricing model, not evidence of an executable trading advantage or a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. The methods and sample restrictions are described below; detailed numerical and robustness results appear in the online supplement.
