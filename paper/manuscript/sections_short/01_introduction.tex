\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short_intro}

Option prices depend on parameters that are estimated rather than observed. Bayesian pricing can account for this uncertainty by averaging option values over the posterior distribution of the estimated parameters, rather than using only a point estimate. This idea is established in Bayesian option pricing and forecasting \citep{guidolin2003,romboutsstentoft2014,guptareisinger2014}. Here we ask a more specific question: when does this averaging materially change the option value?

Arithmetic-average Asian options provide a useful setting because their exposure to volatility changes with moneyness, maturity, and the fraction of the averaging period already fixed. The numerical literature has developed efficient methods for this path dependence, including geometric-average control variates and conditioning approximations \citep{kemnavorst1990,curran1994}. Commodity applications additionally require a term structure of futures prices and, in richer specifications, stochastic convenience yields, jumps, or stochastic volatility \citep{schwartz1997,shirayatakahashi2011,ewaldwu2023}. We do not propose a new Asian-option pricing model. Instead, we hold the pricing model fixed and compare the effect of averaging over the volatility posterior with the effect of changing the volatility input.

These comparisons answer different questions. The first asks how much the price changes when we average over the posterior rather than substitute posterior mean volatility. The second asks whether historical volatility and option-implied volatility lead to different pricing errors. The first depends on the nonlinearity of the pricing function; the second depends on the information used to estimate volatility.

Let \(C^Q(\sigma)\) be the risk-neutral option value conditional on volatility \(\sigma\), let \(\mathcal D\) denote the historical return data, and let \(\bar\sigma=\mathbb E[\sigma\mid\mathcal D]\). The posterior-integrated price and posterior-mean plug-in price are
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}=\mathbb E[C^Q(\sigma)\mid\mathcal D],
 \qquad
 C_{PM}=C^Q(\bar\sigma).
 \label{eq:short_pi_pm}
\end{equation}
A second-order expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 C_{PI}-C_{PM}
 \approx
 \frac12 C^{Q\prime\prime}(\bar\sigma)
 \operatorname{Var}(\sigma\mid\mathcal D).
 \label{eq:short_taylor}
\end{equation}
The PI--PM difference is therefore larger when the volatility posterior is more dispersed and the option price is more curved as a function of volatility. By contrast, the spread of the posterior distribution of model prices is locally proportional to the posterior standard deviation. PI and PM can be close even when the possible model prices differ substantially.

The empirical application uses WTI Average Price Options (APOs), whose payoff depends on the arithmetic average of daily first-nearby WTI futures settlements. WTI allows us to compare historical volatility with implied volatility obtained from both APOs and standard monthly WTI futures options. To estimate implied volatility, we use only option observations from dates earlier than the date being priced. We then reprice each APO using its valuation-date futures curve, realized fixings, and discount factor. Historical-volatility estimators use returns available under their stated valuation-date cutoff.

Three findings organize the paper. First, the synthetic experiment supports Equation~\eqref{eq:short_taylor}: the PI--PM difference can be large with few historical observations, strong curvature of the option price with respect to volatility, and few realized fixings. Second, PI and PM are almost identical in the October-2026 WTI sample, although the posterior distribution of model prices is much wider than the difference between them. Third, prior option-implied volatility inputs produce substantially lower out-of-sample pricing errors than the historical-volatility benchmarks evaluated here. Rolling-window, exponentially weighted, and horizon-matched GARCH(1,1) estimates do not achieve similar pricing errors. Carrying forward a contract's previous implied volatility shows that a fitted smile is not needed for this result. We also find lower errors using implied volatility from standard monthly WTI futures options, rather than APOs.

These results do not measure a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Under a simple pricing model, implied volatility can reflect risk premia, omitted market dynamics, liquidity, and settlement conventions. Our conclusion is limited to the methods and data studied: implied volatility estimated from earlier option observations produces lower subsequent APO pricing errors than the historical-volatility inputs tested, while integrating the historical-volatility posterior changes the mean model price very little.
