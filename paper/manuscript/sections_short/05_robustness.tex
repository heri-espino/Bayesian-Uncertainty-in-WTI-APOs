\section{Robustness and interpretation}\label{sec:short_robustness}

The online supplement reports the full robustness analyses: historical-volatility benchmarks (Section S1), numerical accuracy and posterior calibration (Section S2), liquidity restrictions (Section S3), and LO volatility mapping and tree refinement (Section S4).

The historical-versus-implied comparison is robust to the tested changes in the return model. Standardized Student-\(t\) innovations modestly affect historical-volatility estimates but leave the PI--PM difference small relative to model-to-market pricing errors. Reconstructing the historical return series contract by contract from first-nearby CL settlements produces a posterior volatility estimate close to the continuous/front-month proxy. On the 2,381 out-of-sample observations, the reconstructed historical benchmark has MAE 2.6468 and RMSE 3.4451, compared with 2.6187 and 3.4090 for the baseline historical series.

The small PI--PM difference is not a numerical artifact. High-precision Monte Carlo, independently scrambled Sobol simulation, and Curran conditioning agree at a scale well below the observed differences. Simulation-based calibration provides a separate check on posterior implementation. We also examine the posterior root mean square (RMS) volatility,
\[
 \sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}}=\sqrt{\mathbb E[\sigma^2\mid\mathcal D]},
\]
which we compare with posterior mean volatility using the same Curran approximation. The two inputs give nearly the same pooled pricing errors. Detailed numerical and calibration results appear in Online Supplement Section S2 and Table S2.

The external LO comparison is stable under two scalar-volatility mappings: fixing-count weighted RMS and variance matching for the remaining average. LO implied-volatility estimates also change little when the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein tree is refined from 80 to 160 and 320 steps; the main analysis uses 160 steps. These checks support numerical stability; they do not prove exact tree convergence or that the entire fitted volatility surface is arbitrage-free. Details appear in Online Supplement Section S4 and Table S4.

Liquidity and dependence across contracts still limit the evidence. Many option settlements have zero reported trading volume, especially at longer maturities. The option-implied advantage remains in positive-volume and open-interest-filtered samples, but some expiries contain few positive-volume observations. Valuation-date cluster resampling addresses contemporaneous dependence across contracts, but the 15 APO target dates and 10 external-validation dates do not constitute long independent market histories. The liquidity-restricted results are summarized in Online Supplement Section S3 and Table S3.

The maintained model assumes one futures-curve factor and constant volatility. Multifactor dynamics, stochastic volatility, jumps, or stochastic convenience yields could improve absolute fit, but introducing them would confound the comparison between posterior integration and volatility inputs.

Implied volatility here is the scalar input that matches observed option settlements under the maintained model. It is not a unique structural risk-neutral parameter or a direct estimate of the variance risk premium. The empirical conclusion concerns out-of-sample pricing errors for the methods and observations studied.
