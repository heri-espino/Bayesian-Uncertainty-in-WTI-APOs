\section{Robustness and interpretation}\label{sec:short_robustness}

Alternative physical-return assumptions do not materially change the comparison. A standardized Student-\(t\) model leaves the PI--PM difference small relative to historical-volatility pricing errors. Reconstructing first-nearby CL returns contract by contract gives a similar result: on the 2,381 date-ordered observations, historical PI has MAE 2.6468 and RMSE 3.4451, compared with 2.6187 and 3.4090 using the continuous/front-month return proxy. Further historical-volatility benchmarks appear in Online Supplement Section S1.

The small empirical PI--PM difference is not attributable to numerical noise. High-precision Monte Carlo, independently scrambled Sobol simulation, and Curran conditioning agree on the difference to a precision well below its observed magnitude. Simulation-based calibration provides a separate check on the posterior implementation. The supplement also reports a posterior-RMS plug-in sensitivity (Section S2), alternative mappings from LO-implied volatilities to one APO volatility, and Cox--Ross--Rubinstein tree-refinement checks (Section S4). These numerical checks do not establish an exact arbitrage-free representation of the volatility surface.

The bounded independent-payoff check also establishes that Curran
conditioning is not an exact payoff oracle. In six one-factor numerical
controls, its largest absolute difference from the randomized Sobol
reference is \$0.000922 per barrel; the largest associated 95\%
reference-uncertainty halfwidth is \$0.000154 per barrel. For the
$\rho=0.50$ correlation stress, the largest reference halfwidth is
approximately \$0.001023 per barrel, although its model-price effects
are much larger. A full fixed-history comparison of 513, 1,025, 2,049, and
4,097 volatility-grid nodes preserved all 1,440 synthetic
coverage classifications (456, 313, and 153 covered states
out of 480 for $\rho=1$, $0.75$, and $0.50$).
Against the 4,097-node benchmark, the largest 513-node
PI difference is below \$0.000000005 per barrel and the largest
PM difference is \$0.000056 per barrel, but posterior interval
endpoints differ by up to \$0.005227 per barrel.
A single $\rho=0.75$ endpoint crosses the numerical uncertainty
region of its independent QMC reference at the two finest
grids; its point-reference classification does not change.
The interval-coverage counts are thus stable over this grid
sensitivity, not proof of an exact continuous-grid limit or
real-market calibration. In particular, do not interpret the
smallest \$0.001 decision threshold as exact-payoff accuracy.
The independent-seed follow-up is pilot-informed and was not
preregistered; its hypothetical futures correlations do not constitute
an estimate of market misspecification or validation of exchange marks.

The market evidence has important limits. In the original 2,381-observation comparison, 2,319 option-date observations have zero recorded trading volume. The historical-versus-implied ranking remains under positive-volume and open-interest restrictions, but some expiry-level samples are small (Online Supplement Section S3). Moreover, the main pricing comparison contains only 15 valuation dates and the external comparison only 10. Publication timestamps and revision vintages of the original vendor records remain unverified. Valuation-date cluster resampling preserves dependence among contracts observed together, but cannot eliminate serial dependence across adjacent dates.

A further benchmark-dependence issue is that CME Clearing switched
the WTI Average Price Option end-of-day settlement valuation model
to Curran's model effective May 18, 2020
\citep{cmeclearing2020curran}. Thus a Curran-based repricing benchmark
and model-generated settlement marks need not be independent.
The Barchart \texttt{Latest} field has not been independently tied to
the exchange's final settlement calculation. The historical versus
option-implied comparison should be interpreted as conditional
agreement with available price proxies, not independent validation
of the market's stochastic process.

Finally, the one-factor constant-volatility model excludes richer futures-curve dynamics, stochastic volatility, jumps, and convenience yields. Its implied volatilities are model-equivalent pricing inputs, not uniquely identified risk-neutral diffusion parameters or direct estimates of a variance risk premium. The pricing-error differences apply to these inputs and this model; they do not identify physical and risk-neutral volatility components, trading gains, or the value of a hedging strategy.
