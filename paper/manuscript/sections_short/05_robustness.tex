\section{Robustness and interpretation}\label{sec:short_robustness}

The online supplement contains further checks. Section S1 compares historical-volatility methods. Section S2 checks numerical accuracy and posterior calibration. Section S3 examines liquidity restrictions. Section S4 tests the LO volatility mapping and tree size. Together, these checks show how sensitive the results are to the models, calculations, and data choices.

First, the main pricing-error comparison is similar under other historical-return assumptions. Using standardized Student-\(t\) returns changes the historical-volatility estimates modestly. PI and PM still differ very little compared with their pricing errors against market settlements. Reconstructing the historical return series contract by contract from first-nearby CL settlements produces a posterior volatility estimate close to the continuous/front-month proxy. On the 2,381 out-of-sample observations, the reconstructed historical benchmark has MAE 2.6468 and RMSE 3.4451, compared with 2.6187 and 3.4090 for the baseline historical series.

Second, numerical error does not explain why PI and PM are close. We reprice difficult contracts using high-precision Monte Carlo, scrambled Sobol sequences, and Curran conditioning. The numerical differences between these methods are much smaller than the observed PI--PM differences. Simulation-based calibration provides a separate check on posterior implementation. We also examine the posterior root mean square (RMS) volatility,
\[
 \sigma_{\mathrm{RMS}}=\sqrt{\mathbb E[\sigma^2\mid\mathcal D]},
\]
which we compare with posterior mean volatility using the same Curran approximation. The two inputs give nearly the same pooled pricing errors. Detailed numerical and calibration results appear in Online Supplement Section S2 and Table S2.

Third, the external LO result is similar under two ways of choosing one volatility for the APO model. One uses a fixing-count weighted RMS value. The other chooses the volatility that matches the variance of the remaining average. Their pricing errors are close. LO implied volatilities also change little between trees with 80, 160, and 320 steps. We use 160 steps in the main results. These checks support numerical stability; they do not prove exact tree convergence or that the entire fitted volatility surface is arbitrage-free. Details appear in Online Supplement Section S4 and Table S4.

Liquidity and dependence across contracts still limit the evidence. Many option settlements have zero reported trading volume, especially at longer maturities. The option-implied advantage remains in positive-volume and open-interest-filtered samples, but some expiries contain few positive-volume observations. Valuation-date cluster resampling addresses contemporaneous dependence across contracts, but the 15 APO target dates and 10 external-validation dates do not constitute long independent market histories. The liquidity-restricted results are summarized in Online Supplement Section S3 and Table S3.

The pricing model has one factor and constant volatility. A richer commodity model could include several futures-curve factors, stochastic volatility, jumps, or stochastic convenience yields. Such models might reduce pricing errors. Here, keeping the model fixed makes it possible to compare posterior averaging with the choice of volatility input.

The results do not estimate a variance risk premium or establish a unique risk-neutral volatility. Our implied volatility is the input that makes the chosen model match observed option prices. It may also capture risk premia, missing market dynamics, liquidity, and settlement practices. The conclusion is limited to lower subsequent APO pricing errors for earlier option-implied volatility in the tested sample.
