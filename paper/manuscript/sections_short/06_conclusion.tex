\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:short_conclusion}

We examine two questions in WTI Average Price Option pricing. How much does averaging over the volatility posterior change the model price relative to using posterior mean volatility? And how much do pricing errors change when the volatility input comes from earlier option prices rather than historical returns? The first question is governed locally by posterior variance and curvature of the option-pricing function. The synthetic experiment supports this approximation and shows that the PI--PM difference can be large with short historical samples and strong price curvature.

In the observed WTI sample, PI and PM are almost identical, although the posterior distribution of model prices remains much wider than the difference between them. A small PI--PM difference therefore does not imply little parameter uncertainty in the option value; it means that nonlinear averaging changes the posterior mean price very little in the observed states.

Changing the volatility input has a much larger effect on empirical pricing errors. Prior option-implied volatility produces substantially lower out-of-sample pricing errors than the historical-volatility benchmarks evaluated here. The result is robust to recent-window, EWMA, and horizon-matched GARCH comparisons, does not require a flexible implied-volatility smile, and persists when the volatility input is obtained from a separate vanilla-WTI option market.

These findings are based on relatively few market dates and one deliberately simple pricing model. They do not measure a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. For the WTI APO observations studied here, using prior option-implied volatility reduces pricing errors much more than replacing the posterior-mean plug-in price with the posterior-integrated price.
