\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:short_conclusion}

This paper compares two decisions in pricing WTI Average Price Options: whether to average over the volatility posterior, and whether to use historical or option-implied volatility. A second-order approximation links the first decision to posterior variance and price curvature. In synthetic data, posterior averaging can change the price substantially when the return history is short and curvature is strong.

In the October-2026 WTI sample, posterior averaging barely changes the point price. Yet uncertainty about volatility still produces a much wider range of model prices. A small PI--PM difference is not evidence that parameter uncertainty is negligible.

The source of the volatility input matters much more for the observed pricing errors. Earlier option-implied volatility outperforms the tested historical estimates, including short windows, EWMA, and GARCH. This improvement does not require a fitted smile and also appears when implied volatility comes from standard monthly WTI futures options.

The evidence covers relatively few market dates and one simple pricing model. It does not identify a structural difference between physical and risk-neutral volatility. Within these limits, changing the source of volatility reduces pricing errors much more than changing from PM to PI.
